\section{Threats to Validity}

\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{External Validity}

All replicas run on a single host inside containers over a bridge network, so
the measurements contain no wide-area latency, no real packet loss, and no
multi-machine failure domains. The host is a WSL2 environment, which places a
virtualisation layer between the containers and the underlying hardware.
Cluster sizes are 3--9, the keyspace is 1{,}000 keys, commands are uniform in
size, and the measured phase is 20\,s. The results therefore describe short,
uniform, single-host runs rather than long-lived deployments with skewed access
patterns, large payloads, heterogeneous hardware, or geographically distributed
replicas.

The benchmark also places multiple replicas, the client, and the control
processes on the same host. Consequently, processes may compete for host CPU
resources, and measured resource utilisation can depend on host-level
scheduling and contention. The experiments detect anomalous resource
conditions, but do not provide strict CPU or hardware isolation. Resource
utilisation should therefore be interpreted as an observation under the
evaluated environment rather than as an implementation-independent resource
requirement.

Each replica is allocated two CPUs, while the evaluated processes use
\texttt{GOMAXPROCS=4}. Thus, the configured Go scheduler parallelism exceeds
the nominal container CPU quota. The resulting interaction with host
scheduling and cgroup CPU throttling is part of the measured environment and
may affect both throughput and latency. Repeating the experiments with
\texttt{GOMAXPROCS} matched to the CPU quota would provide an additional
sensitivity check.

Reads are routed through consensus in both protocols and no Raft-specific read
optimisation is enabled. The read comparison is therefore one of equivalent
consensus-facing request paths rather than of each system's fastest possible
read path. A deployment using mechanisms such as leader leases or
\texttt{ReadIndex} could exhibit a different read profile. Similarly, both
systems are measured with identical CPU and memory allocations. This provides
a controlled comparison but does not represent a resource-tuned deployment of
either implementation.

The comparison does not establish that the measured behaviour is representative
of all Raft or EPaxos implementations. The implementation-sensitivity
experiments explicitly demonstrate that batching, persistence, networking,
scheduling, transport handling, configuration defaults, and other
implementation choices can substantially affect measured performance.
Consequently, absolute throughput and latency should be interpreted as
properties of the evaluated implementations and environment rather than as
intrinsic properties of the underlying protocols.

The EPaxos implementation is additionally modified to support the
dependency-set sizes required by the 7- and 9-replica configurations. These
modifications are part of the evaluated implementation and may contribute to
the observed performance at those replica counts. Results at larger cluster
sizes should therefore not be interpreted as isolating the cost of the EPaxos
protocol from the cost of this implementation extension.

The communication experiment introduces delay in the application-level
outbound message path rather than through a physical network emulator. The
injected delay therefore does not reproduce all effects of real networks,
including queueing, packet loss, link-level scheduling, or interactions among
independent flows. In addition, the jitter experiment changes the mean delay
when jitter is increased. Its results consequently cannot isolate the effect
of delay variance from the accompanying change in mean delay. A network-level
experiment using an emulator such as \texttt{tc netem}, or an equivalent
asynchronous delay mechanism, would provide stronger external validity.

The failure and leader-election experiments are conducted with three replicas.
Their recovery times therefore characterise the evaluated three-replica
configuration and should not be interpreted as establishing recovery behaviour
for larger clusters. The failure scenarios are also not symmetric: the Raft
leader-failure experiment removes the replica used as the normal request
endpoint, whereas the Raft follower-failure and EPaxos replica-failure
experiments remove a non-central replica. The results therefore characterise
specific failure modes rather than providing a complete cross-protocol
fault-tolerance comparison.

For Raft, the observed leader-recovery interval also depends on the configured
heartbeat and election-timeout parameters, as well as client timeout and
reconnection behaviour. The reported recovery times should therefore be
interpreted relative to the evaluated timing and request-handling configuration
rather than as protocol-independent bounds on Raft recovery.

Finally, the correctness harness uses deterministic unique-key operations and
compares replica state after execution. It verifies state convergence under
the tested workloads and restart scenarios, but it does not constitute a
formal verification of consensus correctness or establish general
linearizability. In particular, the unique-key workload does not exercise
conflicting operations on the same state in a way that would expose every
possible ordering violation.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity}

Each configuration is measured ten times, and aggregates are reported as means
with sample standard deviation and a $t$-based 95\,\% confidence interval. Ten
repetitions leave substantial uncertainty for a variable system: measuring the
same nominal configuration in four steady-state experiment families produced
means that differed by up to 21\,\% for Raft and 8\,\% for EPaxos, while
confidence intervals overlap in several comparisons whose means differ, such
as at seven and nine replicas. We therefore interpret differences in the
context of observed run-to-run variability and treat overlapping comparisons
as inconclusive rather than as evidence of a small but established effect.

The experiment suite contains many configurations and pairwise comparisons
without a global multiplicity correction. An isolated statistically detectable
difference should therefore not be interpreted as strong evidence for a
general effect. This consideration is particularly important for exploratory
analyses such as regression over workload composition and correlation analyses
within the election experiment.

The election correlation analysis also requires particular caution. The
measured number of failed election attempts is an outcome of the recovery
process and is mechanically related to the injected isolation duration.
Accordingly, the reported correlations characterise associations within the
observed Raft recovery runs rather than providing causal estimates of the
effect of an additional failed election.

The 20\,s measurement window also limits the extent to which the experiments
characterise long-term steady-state behaviour. The reported results should
therefore be understood as measurements over the specified workload and
measurement interval rather than as estimates of indefinite long-running
performance.

The benchmark uses a closed-loop client in which each worker issues its next
request only after the previous request completes. Configured concurrency
therefore represents a maximum number of in-flight requests rather than an
independently controlled arrival rate. Throughput and latency results should
accordingly not be interpreted as a conventional open-loop capacity curve.

Outliers within clean runs are not removed. Runs identified as affected by
environmental interference or measurement failure are investigated separately
and are not excluded solely because their performance is inconvenient for the
comparison. Replacement runs are recorded separately, and the original
observations remain in the raw dataset. All reported aggregates are derived
from the retained per-run observations.
