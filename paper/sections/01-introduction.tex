\section{Introduction}

Distributed systems often replicate the same state across multiple machines in order to provide fault tolerance and continued service in the presence of failures. When a new operation modifies the state of a replicated state machine, replicas must agree on how that operation is ordered and applied. If different replicas apply operations in different orders, their states can diverge, violating the consistency guarantees expected from the system. Consensus is therefore a fundamental problem in distributed systems.

Paxos is one of the foundational consensus algorithms that established a formal approach to reaching agreement among distributed nodes in the presence of failures~\cite{lamport1998parttime}. Despite its theoretical importance, Paxos is notoriously difficult to understand and implement. The original presentation is highly abstract and leaves substantial implementation details to system designers. As a result, practical systems based on consensus algorithms may differ considerably in their architecture and implementation choices.

Raft was introduced with a different design goal: making consensus easier to understand and implement while maintaining strong fault-tolerance guarantees~\cite{ongaro2014raft}. A central feature of Raft is its explicit leader. Client requests that modify replicated state are handled by the leader, which determines the log order and replicates entries to follower replicas before they are committed. In this paper, we refer to this arrangement as \emph{centralised ordering}: the responsibility for determining the order of replicated operations is concentrated in a designated leader, while the other replicas primarily replicate and apply the ordering established by that leader. This structure provides a clear division of responsibilities between the leader and followers and simplifies the coordination required for replicated state machine execution.

The centralised structure of Raft also creates a particular pattern of coordination. Write ordering is concentrated at the leader, and changes in the number of replicas or the offered workload may therefore affect the amount of work performed by the leader and the communication required to commit an operation. Leader failure introduces another distinct condition: because normal request processing depends on the leader, the system must establish a new leader before leader-based processing can resume.

An alternative approach is provided by leaderless consensus. Rather than assigning a single replica the responsibility of coordinating all operations, leaderless protocols allow multiple replicas to participate directly in determining the ordering and dependencies of operations. EPaxos is a representative example of this approach~\cite{moraru2013epaxos}. It exploits the independence of non-conflicting operations so that replicas can sometimes commit operations without relying on a single global ordering point.

The absence of a central leader, however, does not eliminate the costs of consensus. Leaderless protocols require mechanisms for identifying conflicts, tracking dependencies, and coordinating the execution of operations. These mechanisms can introduce additional communication and computational overhead. The resulting performance therefore depends on the characteristics of the workload and system configuration rather than following directly from the presence or absence of a leader. Factors such as operation conflicts, client concurrency, replica count, read/write composition, and failures can affect the observed behaviour of either approach.

These differences motivate an empirical comparison that separates absolute performance from performance response to changing conditions. A system may achieve higher throughput under one configuration while exhibiting a different rate of degradation as the workload or system size changes. Moreover, measured performance is affected not only by the consensus algorithm itself but also by implementation-level mechanisms such as persistence, scheduling, communication, and resource management. Comparing a single implementation of each protocol can therefore conflate algorithmic structure with implementation-specific behaviour.

In this paper, we experimentally compare Raft and EPaxos as representative leader-based and leaderless consensus protocols. Rather than assuming that either approach is universally superior, we examine how their measured behaviour changes under controlled variations in system scale and workload. The evaluation considers both absolute performance and the sensitivity of throughput and latency to changes in replica count, client concurrency, operation conflict rate, and read/write composition. We additionally examine failure behaviour and the effect of implementation choices on the measured results.

We focus on the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{RQ1:} How do the throughput and latency of leader-based and leaderless consensus respond to changes in replica count, client concurrency, conflict rate, and read/write composition?

\item \textbf{RQ2:} How does replica failure affect the availability and performance of the two approaches, and how does leader failure in Raft differ from follower failure?

\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent do implementation-level differences affect the measured performance of Raft and EPaxos?

\end{itemize}

We make the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Reproducible benchmark infrastructure:} We build the Consensus Lab, which runs Raft and EPaxos in a common execution environment behind a common client harness and interface, providing a controlled basis for comparing their measured behaviour.

\item \textbf{Sensitivity-oriented evaluation:} We characterise throughput and latency under controlled changes in replica count, client concurrency, conflict rate, and read/write composition, analysing both absolute measurements and their relative changes across configurations.

\item \textbf{Failure behaviour analysis:} We measure the effects of leader and replica failures on request processing and service availability, distinguishing the effects of Raft leader failure from follower failure and replica failure in EPaxos.

\item \textbf{Implementation-level analysis:} We examine how different implementations of the same consensus approach affect measured performance, allowing implementation-specific effects to be distinguished from observations that are consistent across implementations.

\item \textbf{Open infrastructure:} The lab, experiment configurations, and analysis pipeline are released at \url{https://github.com/Sephy314/Leader-consensus-comparison}, allowing the experiments and analysis to be reproduced and the measured data to be independently re-analysed.

\end{itemize}

To answer these questions, we conduct controlled experiments comparing Raft and EPaxos under comparable system configurations and workloads. The experiments measure throughput, latency, resource consumption, and behaviour under node failures. Performance sensitivity is analysed using measurements across multiple configurations rather than treating a single benchmark configuration as representative of the protocol as a whole. Because the experiments are conducted in a local environment rather than on physically distributed machines, the results should be interpreted as measurements of protocol behaviour under the specified experimental conditions rather than as universal measurements of performance in production-scale distributed systems.
