\section{Introduction}

\label{sec:introduction}

Consensus protocols coordinate replicas so that a replicated state machine
can apply operations consistently despite concurrent execution and replica
failures. Two widely studied design approaches differ in how they establish
this coordination. Leader-based protocols such as Raft~\cite{ongaro2014raft}
direct client operations through an elected leader, whereas leaderless
protocols such as EPaxos~\cite{moraru2013epaxos} allow replicas to propose
operations independently and determine the dependencies required for
consistent execution. Section~\ref{sec:background} develops this distinction
and reviews related empirical work.

These architectural differences motivate empirical comparison, but a direct
protocol-level ranking is difficult to interpret. Measured throughput and
latency depend on replica count, client concurrency, workload composition,
conflict rate, and communication conditions. They are also affected by
implementation mechanisms including persistence, request processing,
scheduling, transport, and message handling. The presence or absence of a
leader is therefore an important structural property, but it does not by
itself determine the performance or failure behaviour of a concrete
implementation. A measured difference between two implementations cannot
automatically be attributed to the consensus algorithm alone.

This distinction matters for experimental studies of consensus: a
benchmark that varies only the protocol while holding a single
implementation and workload fixed cannot establish whether a measured
difference is robust to changes in system conditions or implementation
choices, whereas evaluating multiple implementations and controlled
operating conditions can reveal which observations remain stable and which
are specific to the systems being measured.

In this paper, we experimentally compare Raft and EPaxos as representative
leader-based and leaderless consensus protocols. We measure throughput,
latency, resource utilisation, and failure behaviour under controlled
variation of system and workload conditions, and we additionally evaluate
multiple implementations and matched persistence configurations to examine
whether the observed relationship between the two protocol families remains
stable when implementation-level mechanisms are changed.

We investigate the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{RQ1:} How do throughput and latency change with workload and
system conditions, including read/write ratio, client concurrency, conflict
rate, and replica count?

\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do the evaluated Raft and EPaxos implementations
differ in failure behaviour and in their sensitivity to communication cost?

\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent are the observed performance differences
between Raft and EPaxos attributable to implementation choices rather than
to the protocol families themselves?

\end{itemize}

To answer these questions, we contribute a reproducible experimental
framework that executes both protocol families in a common containerised
environment using a shared client harness and workload generator. The
complete benchmark and analysis artefacts are released at
\url{https://github.com/Sephy314/Leader-consensus-comparison}.

We further contribute a sensitivity-oriented evaluation rather than a
single-configuration benchmark. The experiment suite varies replica count,
client concurrency, read/write composition, conflict rate, failure mode,
and communication conditions, allowing performance relationships to be
examined as functions of controlled system and workload changes.

Finally, we provide an implementation-level analysis using multiple
implementations of the two protocol families and matched persistence
configurations. The results show that implementation mechanisms can
materially change the measured performance relationship and, under matched
storage conditions, can reverse the ordering observed in the baseline
configuration.

The experiments are conducted in a local containerised environment.
Accordingly, the reported values are measurements of the evaluated
implementations under the specified experimental conditions rather than
universal estimates of production performance. In particular, the
communication experiments provide controlled evidence about sensitivity to
added communication cost, but do not constitute a physical wide-area
deployment.

Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews consensus protocols and related work.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the Consensus Lab and experimental
