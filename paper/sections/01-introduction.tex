\section{Introduction}

Distributed systems often replicate state across multiple machines to provide
fault tolerance and continued service in the presence of failures. When a
replicated state machine processes an operation, its replicas must coordinate
to maintain a consistent state despite concurrent execution and failures.
Consensus protocols provide the coordination mechanisms required for this
process and are therefore fundamental to many distributed systems.

Paxos is one of the foundational consensus algorithms for reaching agreement
among distributed nodes in the presence of failures~\cite{lamport1998parttime}.
Although its formulation is influential, the original presentation is
highly abstract and leaves substantial implementation decisions to system
designers. Consequently, systems implementing consensus can differ
considerably in their execution paths, storage mechanisms, communication
patterns, and resource requirements even when they implement the same
underlying protocol family.

Raft was introduced with an emphasis on understandability and
implementability while providing fault-tolerant consensus
\cite{ongaro2014raft}. A central feature of Raft is its explicit leader.
Client operations are submitted through the leader, which determines the
order of log entries and coordinates their replication before commitment.
This leader-based structure provides a clear separation between request
coordination and replica execution. It also creates a characteristic
coordination pattern: normal request processing is concentrated at the
leader, and leader failure requires an election before leader-based request
processing can resume.

EPaxos provides a contrasting leaderless approach
\cite{moraru2013epaxos}. Rather than relying on a single replica to determine
a global order for all operations, EPaxos allows replicas to propose
operations and determine dependencies among them. Operations that do not
conflict can therefore avoid some of the coordination associated with a
single global ordering point. This structure does not eliminate consensus
overhead, however. Dependency tracking, conflict detection, and additional
coordination can introduce their own communication and computational costs.

These architectural differences motivate an empirical comparison, but they
also make a simple protocol-level performance ranking difficult to
interpret. A protocol may perform differently as the number of replicas,
client concurrency, operation conflicts, or communication delay changes.
Furthermore, measured performance is affected by implementation-level
mechanisms such as persistence, scheduling, message handling, and resource
management. As a result, a performance difference observed between two
implementations cannot automatically be attributed to the consensus
algorithm alone.

This distinction is particularly important when comparing leader-based and
leaderless consensus. The presence of a leader provides a structural
difference in how requests are coordinated, but it does not by itself
determine the measured throughput or latency of a concrete implementation.
Conversely, an observed performance difference between two implementations
may arise from the interaction between protocol structure and implementation
mechanisms. An experimental comparison should therefore examine both
absolute performance and how that performance changes across controlled
conditions.

In this paper, we experimentally compare Raft and EPaxos as representative
leader-based and leaderless consensus protocols. We evaluate throughput,
latency, resource utilisation, and failure behaviour under controlled
variations in replica count, client concurrency, operation conflict rate,
read/write composition, and communication conditions. We additionally
compare multiple implementations to examine how implementation choices
affect the measured relationship between the two protocol families.

We focus on the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{RQ1:} How do the measured throughput and latency of the
evaluated leader-based and leaderless implementations change with replica
count, client concurrency, conflict rate, and read/write composition?

\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do the evaluated implementations behave under
replica and leader failures, particularly with respect to availability
gaps and request failures?

\item \textbf{RQ3:} How do communication conditions and implementation
choices affect the measured performance differences between Raft and
EPaxos?

\end{itemize}

We make the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Reproducible benchmark infrastructure:} We build the
Consensus Lab, which executes Raft and EPaxos in a common containerised
environment through a common client harness and benchmark-facing interface,
providing a controlled basis for measuring their behaviour.

\item \textbf{Sensitivity-oriented evaluation:} We measure throughput and
latency across controlled variations in replica count, client concurrency,
conflict rate, read/write composition, and communication conditions rather
than relying on a single benchmark configuration.

\item \textbf{Failure behaviour analysis:} We characterise request
processing and availability under Raft leader failure, Raft follower
failure, and EPaxos replica failure, separating leader-election effects
from replica-failure observations.

\item \textbf{Implementation-level analysis:} We evaluate multiple
implementations of the two protocol families in selected experiments to
quantify the extent to which implementation choices affect the measured
performance relationship.

\item \textbf{Open infrastructure:} We release the lab, experiment
configurations, and analysis pipeline at
\url{https://github.com/Sephy314/Leader-consensus-comparison}, allowing the
experimental procedure and processed results to be independently
re-analysed.

\end{itemize}

The experiments use a common containerised execution environment and the
same client workload model for the evaluated implementations. Performance
measurements are based on repeated runs across controlled configurations,
with raw per-run observations retained for independent analysis. Because the
experiments are conducted in a local environment rather than across
physically distributed machines, the results are measurements of the
evaluated implementations under the specified experimental conditions and
should not be interpreted as universal production-performance estimates.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background}
provides background on distributed consensus and discusses leader-based and

describes the Consensus Lab, the implementations under test, the client
harness and workload model, the metrics, and the experiment suite.
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} presents the experimental results on throughput,

conditions, and implementation differences. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
interprets the results with respect to the research questions and discusses
the observed trade-offs between the evaluated implementations. Section
~\ref{sec:threats} discusses threats to the validity of the measurements.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises the findings.
