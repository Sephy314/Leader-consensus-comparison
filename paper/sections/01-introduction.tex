\section{Introduction}
Consensus protocols coordinate replicas so that a replicated state machine
applies operations in a consistent order. The two dominant design families
contrast in how they establish that order: leader-based protocols such as
Raft~\cite{ongaro2014raft} direct client operations through an elected
leader, whereas leaderless protocols such as
EPaxos~\cite{moraru2013epaxos} let replicas propose operations independently
and determine dependencies among them. Section~\ref{sec:background} develops
this contrast and the related empirical work.

These architectural differences motivate empirical comparison, but a simple
protocol-level ranking is difficult to interpret. Measured performance
depends on replica count, client concurrency, workload composition, conflict
rate, and communication conditions, and it is also affected by implementation
mechanisms such as persistence, scheduling, transport, and message handling.
The presence of a leader is a structural difference, but it does not by
itself determine the throughput, latency, or failure behaviour of a concrete
implementation, so a measured difference between two implementations cannot
automatically be attributed to the consensus algorithm.

In this paper, we experimentally compare Raft and EPaxos as representative
leader-based and leaderless protocols, measuring throughput, latency,
resource utilisation, and failure behaviour under controlled variation, and
using multiple implementations and matched persistence configurations to test
whether observed differences hold across implementation choices.

We focus on the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How do throughput and latency change with workload
and system conditions, including read/write ratio, client concurrency,
conflict rate, and replica count?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} How do Raft and EPaxos differ in failure behaviour
and in their sensitivity to communication cost?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent are the observed performance
differences between Raft and EPaxos attributable to implementation choices
rather than to the protocol families themselves?
\end{itemize}

We make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reproducible benchmark infrastructure:} the Consensus Lab runs
Raft and EPaxos in a common containerised environment through a common client
harness and benchmark-facing interface; the lab, configurations, and analysis
pipeline are released at
\url{https://github.com/Sephy314/Leader-consensus-comparison}.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity-oriented evaluation:} throughput and latency are
measured across controlled variations in replica count, client concurrency,
conflict rate, read/write composition, and communication conditions, so the
evaluation examines changes across conditions rather than a single
configuration.
\item \textbf{Failure and communication analysis:} we characterise
availability under Raft leader failure, Raft follower failure, and EPaxos
replica failure, and separate sensitivity to communication cost applied inside
the implementations from the same nominal cost applied outside them.
\item \textbf{Implementation-level analysis:} multiple implementations of the
two protocol families are evaluated, including matched persistence
configurations; the results show that implementation mechanisms can
materially change, and can reverse, the measured relationship between Raft
and EPaxos.
\end{itemize}

The experiments run in a local containerised environment. The results are
therefore measurements of the evaluated implementations under the specified
conditions rather than universal production-performance estimates, as
Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses in detail.

Section~\ref{sec:background} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:setup}
describes the Consensus Lab and the experiment suite,
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} presents the results,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them with respect to the research
questions, and Sections~\ref{sec:threats} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss
threats to validity and conclude.
