\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Leader-Based vs Leaderless Behaviour}

The scaling experiment shows that the EPaxos advantage over Raft is
significant only at three and five replicas (median ratios 1.22 and 1.73,
$p<0.001$); at seven and nine replicas no significant difference was
detected, and the EPaxos decline at those sizes is accounted for by request
timeouts rather than by slower successful requests
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Median latency was flat for EPaxos
(5.4--5.6\,ms) across replica counts while Raft grew from 6.9 to 14.6\,ms,
and at concurrency 256 EPaxos measured 41{,}077\,req/s against
19{,}333\,req/s for Raft with p50 5.6\,ms against 12.1\,ms. The larger
EPaxos response at high concurrency is consistent with its ability to
accept proposals at multiple replicas, whereas the evaluated Raft
configuration directs all requests through the elected leader; the
experiments do not isolate this mechanism from other implementation-level
costs.

\subsection{RQ2: Workload and Network Sensitivity}

Write ratio and conflict ratio produced no systematic throughput trend
(Kruskal--Wallis tests not significant for either implementation), which is
consistent with reads being routed through the same consensus path as
writes. Under injected one-way delay, the four implementations differed
sharply: at 10\,ms, throughput fell by 72--96\,\%, and etcd Raft collapsed
by 94\,\% at 1\,ms already. The etcd collapse reflects its per-message
sender serialisation under the injection (Section~\ref{sec:setup}), so the
communication-cost results measure each implementation's send-path
structure rather than a protocol-level latency-sensitivity ranking. Jitter
had a much weaker effect than the fixed cost (24--36\,\% at 100\,\% jitter
on a 5\,ms base), consistent with the increase in mean delay it introduces.

Failure behaviour differed by the role of the failed replica rather than by
protocol alone: losing the Raft leader produced availability gaps of
1.81--4.74\,s, whereas losing a Raft follower or an EPaxos replica produced
gaps below 0.1\,s. The measured failed-election count was strongly
associated with the gap ($r=0.90$), but this is partly mechanical because
the injection duration grows with the target; the residual recovery
association ($r=0.41$) is reported descriptively.

\subsection{RQ3: Implementation Sensitivity}

The implementation comparison is the strongest qualification to any direct
protocol ranking. At zero injected cost, the measured throughputs were
6{,}420\,req/s for etcd Raft, 5{,}527\,req/s for the original EPaxos,
4{,}502\,req/s for HashiCorp Raft, and 4{,}346\,req/s for nvb EPaxos: the
baseline ordering was not determined by whether an implementation was
leader-based or leaderless. Under added delay the divergence grew, with
HashiCorp Raft at 1{,}280\,req/s, nvb EPaxos at 833\,req/s, the original
EPaxos at 221\,req/s, and etcd Raft at 45\,req/s at 10\,ms. Implementations
of the same protocol family therefore responded very differently to the
same condition, and an implementation with higher baseline throughput did
not necessarily degrade least.

These differences arise from the complete implementation stack---request
processing, message handling, persistence, scheduling, transport
behaviour---not from the consensus algorithm family alone. The two Raft
implementations differ in storage (BoltDB vs write-ahead log with batched
fsync) and in send-path structure; the two EPaxos implementations differ in
transport (raw TCP vs gRPC) and in fast-path quorum rules. Observed
performance therefore cannot be attributed to the consensus algorithm
family when concrete implementations differ substantially: a single
baseline benchmark can conceal large differences in behaviour under changed
operating conditions. The central finding is not that either family is
intrinsically faster, but that the measured relationship depends jointly on
consensus structure, implementation, workload, replica count, concurrency,
communication conditions, and failure mode.
