\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Throughput and Latency Across Workloads}

RQ1 asks how the measured throughput and latency of the evaluated
leader-based and leaderless implementations change with replica count,
client concurrency, conflict rate, and read/write composition. The clearest
differences were observed for replica count and client concurrency, whereas
the tested conflict and write ratios produced comparatively little systematic
variation.

Increasing the replica count reduced throughput for both implementations.
At three replicas, the measured median throughput was 5{,}291\,req/s for
EPaxos and 4{,}350\,req/s for Raft. At nine replicas, the corresponding
medians were 2{,}002 and 2{,}112\,req/s. Relative to the three-replica
measurements, this represents reductions of approximately 62\,\% for EPaxos
and 51\,\% for Raft. The EPaxos/Raft throughput ratio was 1.22 at three
replicas, 1.73 at five, 1.12 at seven, and 0.95 at nine replicas. The
difference was statistically significant at three and five replicas but not
at seven or nine replicas. Thus, the measured throughput relationship was
not constant as the cluster grew.

The throughput reduction for EPaxos at seven and nine replicas requires
additional interpretation. Nearly every EPaxos run at these sizes contained
30--247 requests that reached the 2\,s client timeout, whereas the
corresponding Raft runs had no such timeouts. The successful EPaxos requests
still had p50 latency of approximately 5.4--5.6\,ms. The closed-loop
concurrency accounting was consistent with the observed throughput reduction
being caused by workers remaining occupied by timed-out requests rather than
by an equivalent increase in the latency of successful requests. The
available instrumentation does not establish the execution-path mechanism
that caused these timeouts. Consequently, the lower EPaxos throughput at
higher replica counts should not be interpreted solely as a scaling
characteristic of the protocol.

Client concurrency produced a different pattern. Throughput increased for
both implementations across the tested range, but EPaxos scaled more
strongly at high concurrency. At concurrency 256, EPaxos reached
41{,}069\,req/s compared with 19{,}278\,req/s for Raft, with p50 latencies
of 5.6 and 12.1\,ms, respectively. This behaviour is consistent with the
evaluated EPaxos implementation accepting proposals through multiple
replicas, whereas the evaluated Raft request path is centred on the elected
leader. However, the experiment does not isolate this protocol mechanism
from implementation-level request processing, scheduling, and transport
costs.

The latency results provide a related but distinct observation. Raft p50
latency increased monotonically from 6.9\,ms at three replicas to 14.6\,ms
at nine replicas, whereas EPaxos remained within 5.4--5.6\,ms across the
same replica counts. At high concurrency, the difference was also visible:
at concurrency 256, EPaxos had a p50 of 5.6\,ms compared with 12.1\,ms for
Raft. At concurrency 1, however, the ordering was reversed, with 2.8\,ms
for Raft and 5.5\,ms for EPaxos. Thus, the measured latency relationship
depends on workload concurrency rather than remaining fixed across the
tested conditions.

In contrast, the write-ratio experiment showed no statistically significant
throughput trend for either implementation. The same was true for the
recorded conflict-ratio experiment. The conflict-validation experiment
further showed that the configured conflict fraction was actually realised
by the workload, so the null result in the original experiment cannot be
explained simply by an incorrectly generated conflict distribution. Three
of the four evaluated implementations showed no systematic throughput
response over the non-degenerate conflict range, while nvb EPaxos showed a
monotonic decline. The latter demonstrates that conflict sensitivity can be
implementation-specific even when the workload itself realises the intended
access pattern.

Overall, RQ1 indicates that replica count and concurrency produced the most
visible changes in the measured performance, while write ratio and conflict
ratio produced weaker or implementation-specific effects. These findings
characterise the evaluated implementations and workload rather than
establishing that the corresponding factors are unimportant to the
underlying consensus protocols.

\subsection{RQ2: Failure and Communication Behaviour}

RQ2 examines the behaviour of the evaluated implementations under replica
failure and increasing communication cost. The failure experiments show a
clear distinction between failure of the Raft leader and failure of a
non-leader replica. Terminating the Raft leader produced write-availability
gaps of 1.81--4.74\,s and 2{,}169--4{,}133 failed requests, whereas
terminating a Raft follower produced gaps of only 0.02--0.04\,s with no
failed requests. EPaxos replica termination produced gaps of
0.02--0.09\,s with 10--106 failed requests.

This difference is consistent with the role of the elected leader in the
evaluated Raft implementation: leader termination requires the system to
elect a replacement before the normal write path can resume, whereas the
tested follower-failure case leaves the current leader in place. The
observed behaviour should nevertheless be interpreted as a property of the
tested failure mechanisms and implementations rather than as a universal
distinction between leader-based and leaderless consensus. The election
experiment provides additional evidence that recovery behaviour is related
to the number of disrupted elections. The measured failed-election count
was strongly associated with the total availability gap ($r=0.90$), but
this association is partly mechanical because the imposed isolation
duration contributes directly to the measured gap. After removing the
isolation interval, the association between failed elections and the
remaining recovery interval was weaker ($r=0.41$). This residual relationship
is therefore treated as descriptive rather than causal.

The communication experiments reveal a second form of sensitivity. The
application-level communication-cost experiment initially showed large
differences between implementations. With 10\,ms of injected one-way
delay, throughput reductions were 72\,\% for HashiCorp Raft, 81\,\% for
nvb EPaxos, 96\,\% for the original EPaxos, and 99.3\,\% for etcd Raft.
The etcd Raft implementation was particularly sensitive, falling from
6{,}420\,req/s without injected delay to 375\,req/s at 1\,ms and
45\,req/s at 10\,ms.

However, the controlled network-delay experiment demonstrates why these
results should not be interpreted directly as protocol-level communication
sensitivity. When the same nominal delay was applied externally using
\texttt{tc/netem}, rather than by inserting sleeps into the implementations'
send paths, all four implementations showed a more comparable response.
At 10\,ms, the implementations retained 18.1\,\% of baseline throughput
for HashiCorp Raft, 17.6\,\% for etcd Raft, 13.8\,\% for original EPaxos,
and 9.0\,\% for nvb EPaxos. Thus, the retained-throughput fractions differed
by approximately a factor of two across the four implementations under the
controlled network condition, compared with a factor of approximately forty
under application-level injection.

The controlled experiment therefore provides the stronger basis for
discussing communication-cost sensitivity. Increasing communication delay
caused a substantial and statistically detectable reduction in throughput
for all implementations, but the relative changes were of a similar order
across the four implementations. In particular, the differences were much
smaller than those produced by the application-level injection mechanism.
This suggests that part of the extreme sensitivity observed in the initial
communication experiment originated from how the delay interacted with the
implementation's message-sending path rather than from the communication
delay itself.

The latency measurements support the same interpretation. At 10\,ms of
controlled delay, p50 latency increased to 36.9\,ms for HashiCorp Raft,
25.7\,ms for etcd Raft, 44.6\,ms for original EPaxos, and 83.0\,ms for nvb
EPaxos. Although the implementations differed, the response was
considerably less divergent than in the application-level experiment. The
controlled network experiment therefore shows that communication cost
affects both throughput and latency, while also demonstrating that the
magnitude of the observed effect depends on where and how the cost is
introduced.

The jitter experiment provides a further qualification. At a 5\,ms base
delay, increasing jitter to 100\,\% reduced throughput by 24\,\% for
HashiCorp Raft and nvb EPaxos, 36\,\% for original EPaxos, and 32\,\% for
etcd Raft. Under the implemented jitter model, however, 100\,\% jitter also
increased the mean injected delay from 5 to 7.5\,ms. Consequently, the
experiment cannot isolate delay variance from the corresponding increase in
mean communication cost. The observed effect is therefore evidence of
sensitivity to the combined change in delay distribution rather than to
jitter variance alone.

Taken together, the failure and communication experiments show that
adverse-condition behaviour depends both on the role of the failed replica
and on the communication mechanism used to impose network cost. The
controlled network experiment also provides evidence that the measured
communication penalty is relatively similar in scale across the evaluated
implementations when the same delay is introduced outside their internal
message-processing paths.

\subsection{RQ3: Implementation-Level Sources of Performance Difference}

RQ3 asks how much of the measured performance difference between Raft and
EPaxos is attributable to the concrete implementations rather than to the
consensus algorithm families themselves. The results from the implementation,
persistence, and communication experiments consistently indicate that the
measured relationship depends substantially on implementation details.

The controlled communication-delay family provides the primary basis for
comparing implementation sensitivity to communication cost. At zero
applied delay, the measured median throughputs were 6{,}479\,req/s for
etcd Raft, 5{,}486\,req/s for original EPaxos, 4{,}556\,req/s for HashiCorp
Raft, and 4{,}475\,req/s for nvb EPaxos. The baseline ordering therefore did
not correspond directly to the leader-based versus leaderless classification.
Moreover, the implementation with the highest zero-delay throughput was not
the implementation retaining the highest throughput after communication delay
was introduced. At 10\,ms, HashiCorp Raft retained the largest fraction of
its baseline throughput, whereas etcd Raft had the highest zero-delay
throughput.

The persistence-matching experiment provides particularly direct evidence
for implementation sensitivity. The recorded baseline compares a durable
HashiCorp Raft implementation with an in-memory original EPaxos
implementation. When both were evaluated in memory, HashiCorp Raft reached
29{,}660\,req/s compared with 5{,}585\,req/s for original EPaxos at three
replicas. When both were evaluated with durable storage, the corresponding
throughputs were 4{,}425 and 2{,}005\,req/s. The same direction was observed
for the second implementation pair: at three replicas, in-memory etcd Raft
measured 12{,}884\,req/s compared with 4{,}226\,req/s for nvb EPaxos, a
3.0$\times$ difference. At five replicas the matched comparisons were also
separated: 4.4$\times$ for the HashiCorp Raft/original EPaxos pair in
memory (24{,}573 against 5{,}613\,req/s), 1.8$\times$ for the same pair
with durable storage (3{,}294 against 1{,}838), and 5.3$\times$ for the
etcd Raft/nvb EPaxos pair (8{,}437 against 1{,}600).

The same implementation also changed substantially when its storage mode
changed. At three replicas, durable storage retained only 15\,\% of
HashiCorp Raft's in-memory throughput, 48\,\% for etcd Raft, and 36\,\% for
original EPaxos. At five replicas, the corresponding retention fractions
were 13\,\%, 69\,\%, and 33\,\%. These results show that the recorded
baseline comparison combines consensus behaviour with a substantial
difference in persistence configuration. The observed EPaxos/Raft throughput
ratio in the baseline therefore cannot be treated as an intrinsic property
of the two protocol families.

The communication experiments provide a second example. Application-level
delay produced extreme implementation-dependent behaviour, including the
near-collapse of etcd Raft. When the same nominal delay was applied outside
the implementations using \texttt{tc/netem}, the retained-throughput range
was much narrower. The same implementation could therefore appear highly
sensitive or comparatively resilient depending on the mechanism used to
introduce the communication cost. This difference demonstrates that the
experimental method itself can interact with implementation architecture.

The resource measurements further support this interpretation. Mean
per-replica CPU utilisation was similar between the baseline Raft and EPaxos
measurements, at 36.5\,\% and 35.4\,\%, respectively, while mean peak RSS
was 28.3\,MB for Raft and 46.4\,MB for EPaxos. Across the HashiCorp Raft
scaling configurations, the leader/follower CPU ratio remained below one,
with ratios of 0.61--0.64 at three replicas, 0.70 at five replicas, and
0.73--0.75 at seven and nine replicas. Thus, the elected leader did not
form the CPU hotspot in the evaluated implementation. This observation
cautions against attributing the measured scaling behaviour solely to the
conceptual centralisation of the Raft leader: the concrete implementation
distributed CPU work differently from what a simple leader-as-bottleneck
model would predict.

The combined results therefore support an implementation-sensitive
interpretation of the Raft--EPaxos comparison. Measured throughput and
latency arise from the interaction of consensus structure with persistence,
request processing, scheduling, message handling, transport behaviour,
workload, replica count, concurrency, and failure conditions. Consequently,
an observed performance difference between the evaluated Raft and EPaxos
implementations cannot be attributed to the consensus algorithm family
alone.

At the same time, the experiments do not imply that algorithmic structure
is irrelevant. The asymmetric failure behaviour of the tested Raft
implementation, the different response to client concurrency, and the
different latency scaling with replica count are consistent with mechanisms
arising from the respective consensus designs. Rather, the results indicate
that these protocol-level mechanisms are observed through concrete
implementations, and implementation costs can materially amplify,
attenuate, or even reverse the measured relationship.

\subsection{Implications for Interpreting the Comparison}

The three research questions together show that a consensus comparison
based on a single implementation pair and a single local-network workload
can conflate several distinct effects. RQ1 shows that the measured
relationship changes with workload and system scale. RQ2 shows that failure
role and communication conditions substantially alter the observed
behaviour. RQ3 demonstrates that persistence, message handling, and the
experimental mechanism used to introduce communication cost can change the
comparison itself.

The controlled network experiment is particularly relevant when considering
communication-intensive environments. At 10\,ms of externally applied
one-way delay, all four implementations experienced substantial throughput
reductions, while the retained-throughput fractions remained within a
relatively narrow 9.0--18.1\,\% range. This result demonstrates measurable
differences in communication sensitivity without supporting a universal
claim about the relative WAN performance of EPaxos and Raft. The experiment
uses a single-host environment with artificial delay and does not reproduce
other WAN characteristics such as packet loss, bandwidth constraints, route
asymmetry, or independent host failures.

Similarly, the failure results provide evidence of a measured availability
cost associated with replacing the Raft leader, but they do not establish
that every leader-based system incurs the same cost. Election timeout
configuration, transport implementation, retry policy, and failure
detection can all affect the measured recovery interval. The same principle
applies to EPaxos: the short recovery observed in the tested replica-failure
scenario characterises that implementation and configuration.

For experimental comparisons, the results therefore indicate that
persistence, transport, communication conditions, workload, failure
configuration, and implementation-specific behaviour should be controlled
or explicitly reported when attempting to separate protocol effects from
system-level effects.
