\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Throughput and Latency Across Workloads}

RQ1 asks how the measured throughput and latency of the evaluated
leader-based and leaderless implementations change with replica count,
client concurrency, conflict rate, and read/write composition. The clearest
differences were observed for replica count and client concurrency, whereas
the tested conflict and write ratios produced comparatively little systematic
variation.

Increasing the replica count reduced throughput for both implementations,
but the EPaxos/Raft ratio changed with cluster size rather than remaining
constant, and the difference was statistically detectable only at the three-
and five-replica configurations (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation},
Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The EPaxos reduction at seven and nine replicas
is partly an artefact of client timeouts rather than a property of the
protocol's scaling: each timed-out request occupies a client worker for the
full client timeout, and the closed-loop accounting including that term
matches every run at those sizes. The lower EPaxos throughput at those
sizes should therefore not be read as a scaling characteristic of the
protocol.

Client concurrency produced a different pattern: EPaxos gained considerably
more from high concurrency than Raft, and the gap was widest at the highest
concurrency tested. This is consistent with the evaluated EPaxos
implementation accepting proposals through several replicas while the
evaluated Raft request path is centred on the elected leader, and a
Raft-only follow-up confirmed the replica-scaling penalty persists at
higher concurrency (43--51\,\% lower at nine replicas across c32--c256)
with the leader's CPU utilisation rising with concurrency while remaining
concentrated on the leader. The experiment does not, however, isolate that
protocol mechanism from implementation-level request processing,
scheduling, and transport costs, and the leader approached but did not
exceed its two-core allocation, so the measurements are consistent with a
leader-centred write path without demonstrating a hard CPU capacity
ceiling.

The latency results show the same condition dependence. Raft's p50 latency
rose monotonically with replica count while the EPaxos p50 stayed within a
narrow band, and at the lowest concurrency tested the ordering of the two
reversed. The measured latency relationship therefore depends on the
operating condition rather than being a fixed property of either design.

The write-ratio and conflict-ratio sweeps produced no statistically
significant throughput trend for the evaluated implementations, and the
conflict-validation family shows that this is not an artefact of the
workload failing to realise the configured fraction: the realised hot-key
access pattern matched the configuration exactly, and three of the four
implementations still showed no systematic response over the range in which
the keyspace retains cold keys. The fourth, nvb EPaxos, declined
monotonically with the configured fraction, which makes conflict sensitivity
implementation-specific rather than a property of the protocol family.

These results bear on prior comparisons. Howard and Mortier found
implementation and configuration to matter more than the protocol family
\cite{howard2020raft}, which matches the spread we measure between
implementations of the same family. van Renesse et al. and Tollman et al.
reported that conflict changes the relative performance of the two designs
and degrades the tail under geo-replication \cite{vanrenesse2015paxos,
tollman2021epaxos}; here the configured fraction left the tail as well as the
median unchanged for three of the four implementations (p99 ratios of
0.96--1.09 at 75\,\% against 0\,\%) and moved nvb EPaxos's median and tail
together. A mechanism that needs cross-site or same-key contention would not
appear in this single-host hot-key workload, which is the configuration in
which we can measure it.

Overall, RQ1 indicates that replica count and concurrency produced the most
visible changes in the measured performance, while write ratio and conflict
ratio produced weaker or implementation-specific effects. These findings
characterise the evaluated implementations and workload rather than
establishing that the corresponding factors are unimportant to the
underlying consensus protocols.

\subsection{RQ2: Failure and Communication Behaviour}

RQ2 examines the behaviour of the evaluated implementations under replica
failure and increasing communication cost. Failure of the Raft leader and
failure of a non-leader replica have clearly different consequences in the
tested configuration: leader termination interrupted writes until a
replacement was elected, whereas terminating a Raft follower or an EPaxos
replica produced interruptions about two orders of magnitude shorter
(Table~\ref{tab:failure}).

This is consistent with the role of the elected leader in the evaluated Raft
implementation, but it should be read as a property of the tested failure
mechanisms and implementations rather than as a universal distinction
between leader-based and leaderless consensus. The election experiment shows
that recovery relates to the number of disrupted elections, although the
association with the total availability gap is partly mechanical because the
imposed isolation interval contributes to that gap directly; once the
isolation interval is removed, the residual association with the remaining
recovery interval is weak and is treated as descriptive rather than causal.

The communication experiments reveal a second form of sensitivity. Under
application-level injection the four implementations appeared to differ
sharply, but applying the same nominal delay outside the implementations
with \texttt{tc/netem} compressed the spread of retained throughput from
roughly a factor of forty to a factor of two, and it also changed the
ordering of the implementations. The extreme apparent sensitivity of the
application-level condition therefore originates in how that mechanism
interacts with each implementation's send path rather than in the added
delay as such: because it sleeps per message, an implementation that sends
more messages per request, or that serialises its sends, is affected more
strongly. The controlled condition is the evidence used here for delay
sensitivity; the application-level measurements characterise each
implementation's send path. Communication cost affects latency as well as
throughput under the controlled condition, but less divergently across
implementations than the application-level condition suggested. The jitter
sweep supports the same reading, with the qualification that the implemented
jitter model raises the mean injected delay as well as its variance, so that
experiment cannot separate the two.

Taken together, the failure and communication experiments show that
adverse-condition behaviour depends both on the role of the failed replica
and on the communication mechanism used to impose network cost, and that the
communication penalty is of a similar order across the evaluated
implementations once the same delay is introduced outside their internal
message-processing paths.

\subsection{RQ3: Implementation-Level Sources of Performance Difference}

RQ3 asks how much of the measured performance difference between Raft and
EPaxos is attributable to the concrete implementations rather than to the
consensus algorithm families themselves. The results from the implementation,
persistence, and communication experiments consistently indicate that the
measured relationship depends substantially on implementation details.

The controlled communication-delay family is the primary basis for
comparing implementation sensitivity to communication cost, and its
baseline ordering does not correspond to the leader-based versus leaderless
classification: the implementation with the highest zero-delay throughput is
not the one that retains the most throughput once delay is applied
(Section~\ref{sec:delay-results}).

The persistence-matching family provides particularly direct evidence. The
recorded baseline compares a durable HashiCorp Raft with an in-memory
original EPaxos, and matching the storage mode reverses the ordering at both
replica counts tested, every matched comparison being significant at
$p<0.001$ (Section~\ref{sec:persistence-results},
Table~\ref{tab:persistence}). The cost of persistence is itself
implementation-specific rather than uniform, and the implementation that
loses the largest fraction of its in-memory throughput when its log is
persisted is the one that the recorded comparison placed against an
in-memory EPaxos. The recorded baseline therefore combines consensus
behaviour with a substantial difference in persistence configuration, and
its EPaxos/Raft ratio cannot be treated as an intrinsic property of the two
protocol families.

The resource measurements add a second observation. Mean per-replica CPU
utilisation was similar between the two baseline implementations even though
their throughput differs, and within the Raft scaling runs the elected leader
consumed 2.2 to 3.8 times the CPU of a follower, the ratio growing with
cluster size. The concentration that a leader-centred request path implies
therefore does appear in resource use; what the throughput results do not
show is a clean scaling penalty for it, because the EPaxos measurements at
the largest sizes are confounded by client timeouts
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

The combined results therefore support an implementation-sensitive reading
of the Raft--EPaxos comparison: measured throughput and latency arise from
the interaction of consensus structure with persistence, request processing,
scheduling, message handling, transport, workload, and failure conditions,
so a difference between the evaluated implementations cannot be attributed
to the algorithm family alone. This does not imply that algorithmic
structure is irrelevant. The asymmetric failure behaviour, the different
response to client concurrency, and the different latency scaling with
replica count are all consistent with mechanisms arising from the respective
consensus designs; the results indicate that those mechanisms are observed
through concrete implementations, and that implementation costs can
materially amplify, attenuate, or even reverse the measured relationship.

\subsection{Implications for Interpreting the Comparison}

The three research questions together show that a comparison based on a
single implementation pair and a single local-network workload can conflate
several distinct effects: the measured relationship changes with workload and
system scale, with failure role and communication conditions, and with the
persistence configuration, the message handling, and the mechanism used to
introduce communication cost. The controlled network experiment demonstrates
measurable differences in communication sensitivity without supporting a
universal claim about the relative WAN performance of EPaxos and Raft, and
the failure results show an availability cost for replacing the Raft leader
without establishing that every leader-based system incurs the same cost:
election timeout configuration, transport implementation, retry policy, and
failure detection can all affect the measured recovery interval, and the same
principle applies to the short recovery measured in the tested EPaxos
replica-failure scenario.

For experimental comparisons, the results therefore indicate that
persistence, transport, communication conditions, workload, failure
configuration, and implementation-specific behaviour should be controlled
or explicitly reported when attempting to separate protocol effects from
system-level effects.
