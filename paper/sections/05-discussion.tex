\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

The experimental results show that Raft and EPaxos incur different costs as a consequence of their ordering structures. EPaxos generally achieved higher throughput in the evaluated workloads, while Raft remained competitive at low concurrency and used less memory. The results therefore indicate that the relative behaviour of the two protocols depends on workload intensity, replica scaling, and failure mode rather than on a single steady-state performance characteristic.

\subsection{RQ1: Steady-State Performance}

The throughput results show that EPaxos generally achieved higher throughput across the evaluated read/write configurations. Changing the read/write ratio did not produce a clear systematic change in the relative performance of the two protocols (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-write-ratio}). Client concurrency was a more apparent factor in the observed performance difference (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-concurrency}). Median latency increased with concurrency for both protocols, from 4.1\,ms at concurrency 1 to 13.4\,ms at concurrency 256 for Raft, and from 5.5\,ms to 14.0\,ms for EPaxos. Thus, the latency measurements converged at the highest concurrency even though the throughput difference between the protocols became larger (Figure~\ref{fig:latency-concurrency}).

This behaviour is consistent with the different organisation of write processing in the two protocols. In Raft, committed writes are coordinated through the current leader, concentrating an important part of the write-processing path at a single replica. EPaxos does not require the same centralised ordering path, allowing a larger portion of consensus processing to be handled across multiple replicas. This distinction becomes more relevant as offered concurrency increases, because the Raft leader must continue to handle the central part of the replicated write path.

The results also show that the observed EPaxos advantage is not present at all concurrency levels. At concurrency 1 and 2, the mean throughput of Raft was higher than that of EPaxos, at 256.7 versus 173.9\,req/s and 411.7 versus 339.4\,req/s, respectively. The intervals were separated at concurrency 1 but overlapped at concurrency 2. At higher concurrency levels, the intervals were separated from concurrency 4 onwards except at concurrency 32, where they overlapped. Concurrency 32 was also the default configuration used in the scaling, conflict-ratio, and failure experiments.

When the offered workload is small, the Raft leader has sufficient capacity to handle the workload without the concentration of processing responsibility becoming an observable throughput bottleneck. Under these conditions, the additional coordination and protocol state associated with EPaxos can reduce or eliminate its throughput advantage. As concurrency increases, however, the centralised processing path becomes more relevant to the observed throughput difference.

These results therefore suggest that the cost associated with centralised leadership is workload-dependent. In the evaluated environment, it was more visible when the offered workload approached a level at which the processing capacity of the Raft leader became relevant, rather than appearing as a constant performance penalty across all concurrency levels.

\subsection{RQ2: Scaling and Workload Sensitivity}

Increasing the number of replicas produced different scaling behaviour in latency and throughput. EPaxos maintained comparatively stable latency as the replica count increased, whereas Raft exhibited a more pronounced increase in latency (Table~\ref{tab:latency}). EPaxos throughput nevertheless also decreased as additional replicas were introduced (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). Thus, distributing ordering responsibility does not eliminate the communication and coordination costs associated with replication.

The throughput comparison should be interpreted together with the uncertainty intervals. At three and five replicas, the measured intervals for EPaxos and Raft were separated, whereas the intervals overlapped at seven and nine replicas. The results therefore provide clearer evidence of a throughput difference at the smaller replica counts, while the larger configurations require more cautious interpretation (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-scaling}). The decrease in EPaxos throughput between five and seven replicas was large in terms of the sample means, but the associated variation also increased. It should therefore not be interpreted as a statistically distinct step without considering the reported uncertainty.

The scaling results consequently reveal different forms of cost rather than a single ordering of the two protocols. EPaxos incurs increasing coordination costs as the replica set grows, while Raft retains a central leader whose processing and communication responsibilities remain concentrated at one replica. The results do not indicate that either architecture eliminates the cost of increasing the replica count; instead, the measured effects appear in different performance dimensions.

The conflict-ratio experiment provides a further workload-sensitivity result. Across the evaluated conflict ratios, EPaxos throughput ranged from 4,662 to 5,235\,req/s, while Raft throughput ranged from 3,514 to 4,425\,req/s (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-conflict-ratio}). The changes observed across conflict configurations were not clearly distinguishable from the variation between repetitions. Consequently, the experiment did not reveal a systematic throughput trend associated with the offered conflict ratio. Within the evaluated setup, the measurements therefore do not provide throughput-level evidence that changing the conflict ratio produced a systematic change in the relative performance of the two protocols.

The conflict sweep measures aggregate throughput under different conflict levels, but does not by itself identify the extent to which requests use EPaxos's fast or slow path. Although fast- and slow-path outcomes are recorded for individual runs, they are not included in the aggregated analysis presented here. The absence of a systematic throughput trend with the tested conflict levels should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that conflict sensitivity is absent.

\subsection{RQ3: Failure and Recovery}

The failure experiments show that the effect of replica failure depends strongly on whether the failed replica is the Raft leader. Failures of Raft followers and EPaxos replicas produced only small availability gaps when sufficient replicas remained available to maintain progress. In contrast, direct Raft leader-failure experiments produced a median gap of 2.59 seconds, with observed values ranging from 1.81 to 4.74 seconds (Table~\ref{tab:failure}, Figure~\ref{fig:failure-recovery}).

The larger disruption following a Raft leader failure is consistent with the recovery steps required by the leader-based architecture. After detecting the leader failure, the remaining replicas must proceed through timeout and leader election before a new leader can be established and normal leader-based processing can resume. This creates a period during which requests cannot be processed through the normal leader-based path.

The difference is also visible at the request level. Raft leader-failure experiments produced approximately 2,169--4,133 failed requests, whereas the corresponding EPaxos replica-failure experiments produced approximately 10--106 failed requests. The measured difference therefore involved not only increased latency but also a substantially larger interruption in successfully processed requests under the evaluated failure conditions.

The separate election-injection experiments require a different interpretation (Figure~\ref{fig:election-failure}). The observed isolation gap reached 13.72 seconds, but this value should not be treated as the recovery time of Raft. The experimental procedure intentionally isolates the target for a duration related to the injected election condition, meaning that part of the measured gap is determined by the experimental procedure itself. When recovery is measured from the end of the isolation period, the median recovery time was 1.15 seconds and the maximum was 3.81 seconds. The decoupled recovery measure showed a correlation of $r=0.41$ ($p=0.001$, $n=60$).

The failure results therefore identify leader failure as a distinct recovery cost in the evaluated Raft configuration. The much longer gaps observed in the election-injection experiment should instead be interpreted as a combination of the injected isolation period and subsequent recovery behaviour, rather than as direct measurements of normal leader-failure recovery time.

\subsection{RQ4: Resource Utilisation and Load Distribution}

The resource measurements reveal another difference between the two architectures. Overall CPU utilisation was broadly similar between Raft and EPaxos, indicating that the observed throughput difference cannot be explained simply by one protocol consuming substantially more total CPU resources.

The distribution of CPU utilisation across individual replicas was different, however. In the 9-replica scaling runs, individual observations ranged from approximately 14.2--69.8\% for Raft and 8.9--42.6\% for EPaxos (Figure~\ref{fig:per-replica-cpu}). These observations are consistent with Raft concentrating a greater portion of processing at the current leader, while EPaxos distributes processing more evenly across replicas. This distinction is particularly relevant because averaging across repeated runs can obscure processing concentration when leadership changes between replicas.

The difference in resource distribution provides another observable consequence of the different ordering structures. In Raft, the leader is responsible for a central part of the consensus path, so its resource utilisation can become substantially higher than that of other replicas. EPaxos instead showed a more even distribution of measured CPU utilisation across the replica set. The cost associated with centralised leadership can therefore appear not only in aggregate throughput but also in the distribution of computational load across replicas.

EPaxos, however, incurred a higher memory cost in the evaluated implementation. Its peak memory usage was approximately 1.8 times that of Raft (Figure~\ref{fig:resource-utilisation}). A possible explanation is that EPaxos maintains additional protocol state and metadata associated with distributed ordering and dependency management. The experiment does not isolate the individual sources of memory consumption, however, so the result should be interpreted as an observed resource-level trade-off rather than evidence that a particular protocol component is solely responsible for the difference.

\subsection{Overall Discussion}

Taken together, the results characterise Raft and EPaxos as making different engineering trade-offs rather than establishing one protocol as uniformly superior. EPaxos generally provided higher throughput in the evaluated workloads and maintained comparatively stable latency as the replica count increased. Its processing load was also distributed more evenly across replicas. These observations were accompanied by higher peak memory consumption and a reduction in throughput as the replica set became larger.

Raft, in contrast, remained competitive at low concurrency, where its leader had sufficient capacity to handle the offered workload. Its lower memory consumption also represents a practical resource advantage in the evaluated configuration. At higher concurrency, however, its centralised processing structure was associated with a larger throughput difference relative to EPaxos. Raft also exhibited a distinct recovery path when the current leader failed.

The failure results are particularly relevant to the cost of centralised leadership. A follower failure does not necessarily interrupt Raft's progress while a sufficient quorum remains available, but failure of the leader changes the role responsible for the normal processing path. The system must establish a replacement leader before normal leader-based processing can continue. This produces a failure mode that differs from the failure of a non-leader replica.

The results therefore identify two observable effects associated with centralised leadership in the evaluated Raft implementation. During normal operation, the leader can become a concentration point for request processing as workload increases. During failure, the same central role creates a dependency on establishing a replacement leader before normal processing can resume. EPaxos avoids this particular centralised dependency, but does so with its own measured costs in memory consumption, coordination, and replica scaling.

Overall, the experiments do not establish that EPaxos is universally preferable to Raft. Rather, they show that the architectural choice changes where the costs of consensus appear: Raft concentrates processing and recovery responsibility around the leader, whereas EPaxos distributes more responsibility across replicas while incurring greater protocol-state and coordination costs. The interpretation of the measured performance therefore depends on the conditions under which the systems are compared rather than on a single performance ordering.

The results should also be interpreted within the limitations of the experimental setup. The client is closed-loop, so measurements at the highest concurrency may include client-side limitations that constrain both protocols and compress the observed difference. Measuring the same nominal configuration in four steady-state experiment families produced means that differed by up to 21\% for Raft and 8\% for EPaxos, so differences smaller than this reproducibility band should not be inferred from a single experiment family. Section~\ref{sec:threats} discusses these and the remaining threats to validity.
