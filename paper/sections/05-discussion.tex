\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: Throughput and Latency Across Workloads}

RQ1 asks how the measured performance of the evaluated leader-based and
leaderless implementations changes with replica count, client concurrency,
conflict rate, and read/write composition. Replica count and concurrency
produced the clearest differences. With three replicas, EPaxos achieved
5{,}292\,req/s compared with 4{,}351\,req/s for Raft, and with five
replicas the corresponding medians were 5{,}443 and 3{,}150\,req/s.
The differences were statistically significant at both configurations
($p<0.001$), whereas no significant difference was detected at seven or
nine replicas. At the latter replica counts, EPaxos throughput was reduced
by request timeouts, while the p50 latency of successful requests remained
between 5.4 and 5.6\,ms. Raft p50 latency increased from 6.9\,ms at three
replicas to 14.6\,ms at nine replicas.

Client concurrency produced a similar divergence. At concurrency 256,
EPaxos reached 41{,}077\,req/s compared with 19{,}333\,req/s for Raft,
with p50 latencies of 5.6 and 12.1\,ms, respectively. This behaviour is
consistent with the evaluated EPaxos configuration accepting proposals at
multiple replicas, whereas requests in the evaluated Raft configuration
are directed through the elected leader. The experiment does not isolate
this mechanism from other implementation-level costs.

In contrast, varying conflict rate and read/write composition did not
produce a systematic throughput trend in the evaluated range. Kruskal--Wallis
tests were not significant for either implementation in the conflict-ratio
experiment or the write-ratio experiment. Thus, under the workload and
consensus path used here, the measured throughput differences were more
sensitive to replica count and client concurrency than to the tested
changes in conflict or read/write composition. These results describe the
evaluated implementations and should not be interpreted as establishing
that conflict rate or read/write composition is irrelevant to the
underlying protocols.

\subsection{RQ2: Failure and Communication Behaviour}

RQ2 examines behaviour under replica and leader failures and under injected
communication delay. Failure behaviour depended strongly on which replica
failed. Killing the Raft leader produced availability gaps of
1.81--4.74\,s and 2{,}169--4{,}133 failed requests, whereas killing a
Raft follower produced gaps of only 0.02--0.04\,s with no failed
requests. EPaxos replica failures produced gaps of 0.02--0.09\,s with
10--106 failed requests. The difference between Raft leader and follower
failures is therefore visible in the measured availability behaviour of
the evaluated implementation. The failed-election count was strongly
associated with the observed availability gap ($r=0.90$), although this
relationship is partly mechanical because the isolation duration increased
with the failure target. The residual recovery association ($r=0.41$) is
therefore reported descriptively rather than as evidence of a causal
relationship.

Injected communication delay produced substantial performance changes,
but the magnitude depended strongly on the concrete implementation. At
10\,ms of one-way injected delay, throughput decreased by 72--96\,\%
across the four implementations. The etcd Raft implementation decreased
from 6{,}420\,req/s at zero delay to 375\,req/s at 1\,ms and
45\,req/s at 10\,ms. This behaviour is associated with the per-message
sender serialisation exposed by the application-level delay injection,
so the result characterises the interaction between the implementation's
send path and the experimental condition rather than establishing a
protocol-level latency-sensitivity ranking.

Jitter produced a smaller effect than the fixed communication cost. With
a 5\,ms base delay, throughput changes at 100\,\% jitter were in the
24--36\,\% range across the evaluated implementations. The observed
effect is consistent with jitter changing the distribution of injected
message delay while retaining a comparable mean-delay scale. Together,
the failure and communication experiments show that availability and
performance under adverse conditions depend on both the failure role and
the communication mechanisms exercised by the concrete implementation.

\subsection{RQ3: Implementation-Level Sources of Performance Difference}

RQ3 asks how much of the measured performance difference between Raft and
EPaxos is attributable to the concrete implementation rather than to the
consensus algorithm family. The four-implementation experiment shows that
the relationship between the two protocol families changes substantially
with implementation and operating condition. At zero injected delay, the
measured throughputs were 6{,}420\,req/s for etcd Raft, 5{,}527\,req/s
for the original EPaxos, 4{,}502\,req/s for HashiCorp Raft, and
4{,}346\,req/s for nvb EPaxos. The baseline ordering therefore did not
follow the leader-based versus leaderless classification.

The differences became larger under communication delay. At 10\,ms,
HashiCorp Raft measured 1{,}280\,req/s, nvb EPaxos 833\,req/s, original
EPaxos 221\,req/s, and etcd Raft 45\,req/s. Implementations belonging to
the same protocol family consequently exhibited substantially different
responses to the same communication condition. An implementation with
higher baseline throughput also did not necessarily retain the highest
throughput after delay was introduced.

These observations indicate that the measured performance relationship
contains contributions from the complete implementation stack, including
request processing, message handling, persistence, scheduling, and
transport behaviour. The evaluated Raft implementations differ in storage
and message-handling mechanisms, while the EPaxos implementations also
differ in their transport and request-processing paths. Consequently, the
observed differences cannot be attributed to the consensus algorithm
family alone when comparing these concrete implementations. The results
instead support an implementation-sensitive interpretation: measured
performance depends jointly on consensus structure, implementation,
workload, replica count, concurrency, communication conditions, and
failure mode.
