\begin{abstract}
Leader-based consensus protocols such as Raft concentrate the ordering of
replicated writes at a single elected leader, whereas leaderless protocols
such as EPaxos distribute ordering across replicas. This paper measures what
that difference costs in practice. Both protocols are evaluated under one
benchmark methodology: the same workload generator, identical CPU and memory
allocations, the same failure injection, and the same metrics, across seven
experiment families with ten independent repetitions each and replica counts
from three to nine, on a single containerised host. EPaxos sustained a higher
mean throughput than Raft at every replica count tested (1.29$\times$ to
1.75$\times$, largest at five replicas) and a lower median latency at every
replica count; the 95\,\% intervals of the means are separated at three and
five replicas but overlap at seven and nine. Throughput decreased for both
protocols as replicas were added (57\,\% for EPaxos and 50\,\% for Raft), so
the scaling penalty is not specific to leader-based ordering. Aggregate CPU
utilisation was similar for the two protocols, but its distribution was not:
Raft concentrated utilisation on the current leader, while EPaxos spread it
more evenly. Failure behaviour differed by failure role rather than by protocol
alone: losing the Raft leader cost seconds of write availability and thousands
of failed requests, whereas losing a Raft follower or an EPaxos replica cost
tens of milliseconds. The write-ratio and conflict-ratio sweeps showed no
systematic trend for either protocol, and EPaxos used about 1.8$\times$ the
peak memory of Raft in the evaluated implementation.

To separate protocol-level behaviour from implementation choices, each
protocol is also evaluated through a second, independent implementation
(etcd Raft beside HashiCorp Raft, nvb EPaxos beside the original EPaxos).
The four implementations differed sharply in their sensitivity to added
one-way communication cost: at 10\,ms, throughput fell by 72--96\,\% across
the implementations, and the etcd Raft implementation collapsed by 94\,\%
at 1\,ms already, so absolute performance is a property of the
implementation stack, not of the consensus algorithm alone. A separate
correctness harness verifies, independently of the performance benchmark,
that every implementation performs consistent consensus execution: all 18
deterministic tests (concurrency 1/8/32 plus leader, follower, and replica
failures) passed, with no missing or duplicate application and agreement
among surviving replicas. The two protocols are represented by different
implementations and were measured on a single local host, so these results
should not be read as universal properties of either protocol.
\end{abstract}
