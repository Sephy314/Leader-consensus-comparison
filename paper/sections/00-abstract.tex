\begin{abstract}

Leader-based and leaderless consensus protocols differ in how replicas
coordinate operations and recover from failures. This study experimentally
examines these differences using Raft and EPaxos as representative
leader-based and leaderless designs. We evaluate throughput, latency, and
failure behaviour across replica count, client concurrency, read/write
composition, conflict rate, and communication cost, while also examining
implementation sensitivity through multiple implementations and matched
persistence configurations.

The results show that performance differences are strongly
condition-dependent. Replica count and client concurrency produce the most
pronounced differences, while read/write composition and conflict rate
produce weaker or implementation-specific effects. The evaluated Raft
configuration concentrates request processing at the elected leader and
exhibits a distinct recovery gap following leader failure, whereas the
evaluated EPaxos configurations distribute proposal processing across
replicas. However, the magnitude of these differences depends substantially
on implementation and persistence choices.

Matched persistence experiments can reverse the performance ordering
observed under the baseline configuration, while the communication
experiments show that the measured delay sensitivity depends on the
mechanism used to impose the delay and on implementation-level message
processing. These results therefore do not
support a universal performance ranking between Raft and EPaxos. Instead,
they show that observed differences emerge from the interaction between
consensus structure, implementation, workload, communication conditions,
and failure mechanisms.

The study consequently argues that empirical comparisons of consensus
protocols should distinguish architectural effects from implementation
effects and evaluate performance across controlled operating conditions.
\end{abstract}
