\section{Evaluation}

\label{sec:evaluation}

Unless otherwise stated, reported point estimates are medians over ten
independent repetitions, results use three replicas, 100\,\% writes, and
client concurrency 32, and results describe the evaluated implementations
under the experimental configuration of Section~\ref{sec:setup}. All
figures are generated from the processed benchmark data by
\texttt{lab/report/figures.py} and show medians across repetitions, with
individual repetition values overlaid where available. Differences should
not be interpreted as protocol-level causal effects unless the experiment
directly isolates the corresponding mechanism.

\subsection{Throughput and Scalability}

Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-scaling} shows measured throughput as the
number of replicas increases. Throughput decreased with replica count for
both evaluated implementations. At three replicas, the measured median was
5{,}291\,req/s for EPaxos and 4{,}350\,req/s for Raft. At five replicas,
the medians were 5{,}442 and 3{,}148\,req/s, respectively, while at nine
replicas they were 2{,}002 and 2{,}112\,req/s (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}).
Relative to the three-replica measurement within this experiment family,
the nine-replica median was 62\,\% lower for EPaxos and 51\,\% lower for
Raft. The ratio of the two medians was 1.22 at three replicas, 1.73 at
five, 1.12 at seven, and 0.95 at nine replicas. Thus, the measured
throughput difference varied with replica count.

We report medians rather than means because a small number of repetitions
stalled. Across the entire dataset (1{,}302 runs), eight repetitions
completed fewer than 10\,\% of the requests of their configuration's
median: two Raft runs (0.3\,\% of Raft runs) and six EPaxos runs (1.0\,\%
of EPaxos runs). Six of the eight were slow from start to finish with no
failed requests, while two EPaxos runs, at seven and nine replicas, showed
persistent request timeouts. The two Raft stalls coincided with elevated
host load (load average 7.0--8.6 against 3.8--4.0 for healthy runs of the
same configuration); the six EPaxos stalls occurred at both low and high
host load. None of the eight runs was flagged by the host-telemetry
contamination rule, so they remain in the dataset. The observed stall
counts (2 of 680 Raft runs and 6 of 610 EPaxos runs) were not significantly
different (Fisher's exact test, $p=0.16$).

A second anomaly affects EPaxos at seven and nine replicas: request
timeouts. Eighteen of the twenty EPaxos runs at those sizes had 30--247
requests time out at the 2{,}000\,ms client timeout (success rates
98.6--99.9\,\%); the other two completed every request, and Raft runs at the
same sizes had no timeouts. Each timeout occupies a
client worker for the full 2\,s, reducing the concurrency available to
successful requests. For a closed-loop client with concurrency $N$, the
accounting

$$
N \approx X_{\mathrm{ok}}W_{\mathrm{ok}}
  + X_{\mathrm{timeout}}W_{\mathrm{timeout}},
$$

where $X_{\mathrm{timeout}}=N_{\mathrm{timeout}}/W$ and
$W_{\mathrm{timeout}}=2\,\mathrm{s}$, with $W$ the measured phase window
(20.0--22.0\,s in these runs), is consistent with every EPaxos run at
these sizes to within 0.8\,\% of $N$. The successful requests themselves
completed in
5.4--5.6\,ms at p50, similar to the three-replica measurements, so the
reduced throughput is accounted for by requests occupying workers until the
timeout rather than by slower successful requests. The available
instrumentation does not establish the specific execution-path mechanism
responsible for the timeouts.

The same nominal configuration (three replicas, 100\,\% writes, and
concurrency 32) also occurs independently in several other steady-state
experiment families, providing an indication of run-to-run variability.
The four family medians were 4{,}350, 4{,}367,
4{,}482, and 4{,}440\,req/s for Raft, and 5{,}291, 5{,}287, 5{,}175, and
5{,}249\,req/s for EPaxos. The EPaxos medians differed by approximately
2\,\% across these measurements, and the Raft medians by approximately
3\,\%. Cross-family variability was therefore of similar magnitude for the
two implementations.

For the scaling family, the difference between the implementations was
statistically significant at three and five replicas (exact Mann--Whitney
$U$ test, uncorrected $p<0.001$), while no statistically significant difference was
detected at seven ($p=0.25$) or nine ($p=0.68$) replicas. The 95\,\%
bootstrap confidence intervals of the medians overlap at seven and nine
replicas (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The measured EPaxos/Raft throughput
difference was therefore statistically significant only at the smaller
cluster sizes in this experiment.

A Raft-only follow-up at client concurrency 128 and 256 (five
repetitions per cell, 40 runs) found the replica-scaling penalty
persists: the nine-replica median was 45\,\% lower at c128 (11{,}856 to
6{,}535\,req/s) and 43\,\% lower at c256 (17{,}325 to 9{,}949\,req/s),
versus 51\,\% at c32 (Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-scaling-conc}); the
r3-to-r9 difference was significant at both levels ($p=0.008$, exact
Mann--Whitney $U$, $n=5$ per group). The leader's CPU utilisation grew
with concurrency (about 53\,\% at c32 to 82\,\% at c128 and 103\,\% at
c256 at three replicas) while followers stayed below 35\,\%. The leader
remained the CPU hotspot under higher offered concurrency, approaching
but not exceeding its two-core allocation, so these measurements do not
demonstrate a hard CPU capacity ceiling.

\subsection{Latency}

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-replica-scaling} shows median (p50) latency as the
number of replicas increases. At three replicas, the median latency was
6.9\,ms for Raft and 5.6\,ms for EPaxos. Raft latency increased to
9.2\,ms at five replicas and 12.0\,ms at seven replicas, reaching
14.6\,ms at nine replicas. EPaxos remained within a relatively narrow
range of 5.4--5.6\,ms across the tested replica counts
(Table~\ref{tab:scaling}b).

The Raft p50 therefore increased monotonically with replica
count, whereas the EPaxos p50 remained within the 5.4--5.6\,ms range.

The p50 values should be read together with the tail: the p99 was 21.4\,ms
for Raft and 23.8\,ms for EPaxos at three replicas, increasing to
26--29\,ms for Raft at seven to nine replicas while remaining at
14--16\,ms for EPaxos. The median of the per-repetition p50 values reduces
the influence of the stalled repetitions described earlier.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\columnwidth]{figures/latency-replica-scaling.pdf}
\caption{Median (p50) latency by replica count at 100\,\% writes and
concurrency 32. Points show individual repetitions; the line marks the
median.}
\label{fig:latency-replica-scaling}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-concurrency} shows median latency across client
concurrency. At concurrency 1, median latency was 2.8\,ms for Raft and
5.5\,ms for EPaxos. At concurrency 32, the corresponding values were
6.9\,ms and 5.6\,ms. At concurrency 256, they were 12.1\,ms and
5.6\,ms.

The relative latency ordering therefore changed with concurrency: Raft had
the lower median at concurrency 1, while EPaxos had the lower median at
concurrency 32 and above.

As with throughput, the same nominal configuration (three replicas,
concurrency 32) is measured by several experiment families. With medians,
the p50 was consistent across families: Raft measured 6.9\,ms in the
scaling family and 6.9\,ms in the concurrency family, while EPaxos
measured 5.6\,ms in both.

For a closed-loop client, Little's law relates mean latency to concurrency
and throughput as $W=N/X$. The reported p50 is the median rather than the
mean, so it need not equal this quantity. At concurrency 256, the Raft p50
of 12.1\,ms was close to $N/X$ (256/19{,}278 $\approx$ 13.3\,ms), while the
EPaxos p50 of 5.6\,ms was below $N/X$ (256/41{,}069 $\approx$ 6.2\,ms).
This difference is compatible with a response-time distribution whose
median is below its mean. We report p50, p95, and p99 in the processed
dataset, using p50 here as the central-tendency summary.

At seven and nine replicas, the EPaxos p50 of 5.4--5.5\,ms was substantially
below $N/X$ (32/2{,}002--2{,}903 $\approx$ 11.0--16.0\,ms). As described in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, this difference is accounted for by the
request timeouts at those sizes: each timeout occupies a worker for the
full 2\,s, and the closed-loop concurrency accounting including the timeout
term is consistent with every EPaxos run. Raft runs at the same sizes had
no timeouts, and their measured $N/X$ values were closer to their reported
p50 latencies.

\subsection{Resource Utilisation}

Figure~\ref{fig:resource-utilisation} summarises the per-replica resource
measurements. Across the evaluated runs, mean per-replica CPU utilisation
was 36.5\,\% for Raft and 35.4\,\% for EPaxos. Mean peak per-replica RSS
was 28.3\,MB for Raft and 46.4\,MB for EPaxos. Mean per-replica network
rates were 451\,kB/s RX and 454\,kB/s TX for Raft, compared with
410\,kB/s RX and 394\,kB/s TX for EPaxos.

Figure~\ref{fig:per-replica-cpu} examines the distribution of CPU
utilisation in the nine-replica scaling experiment. Raft per-replica means
ranged from 16.3\,\% to 29.4\,\%. The replica serving as leader during a
given repetition consumed substantially \emph{more} CPU than the followers.
Taking the leader from each run's own Raft log, the leader/follower ratio of
mean utilisation was 2.2 at three replicas, 2.8 at five, 3.3 at seven and 3.8
at nine replicas, using the medians over the ten repetitions, so the leader's
CPU cost grew relative to each follower as the cluster grew. (The leader's
share of the cluster's total CPU falls as replicas are added; it is the
per-follower ratio that rises.)

This is consistent with the request path of the evaluated HashiCorp Raft
implementation: the leader serves the benchmark client, applies every command,
persists its own log, and broadcasts to every peer, whereas a follower receives,
persists, and applies. A CPU profile of one three-replica run shows the same
shape of work on both roles, about two thirds of the samples going to system
calls and lock waits on the persistence path, with the leader differing from a
follower chiefly in how much of that work it performs. The instrumentation does
not separate the persistence, application, and transport contributions, but the
direction is unambiguous: the elected leader was the CPU hotspot in the
evaluated implementation, and its relative cost grew with the number of peers
it serves.

\subsection{Failure Recovery Behaviour}

Failure outcomes differed by failure type (Table~\ref{tab:failure},
Figure~\ref{fig:failure-recovery}). Killing the Raft leader produced a
write-availability gap of 1.81--4.74\,s and 2{,}169--4{,}133 failed
requests. Killing a Raft follower produced a gap of 0.02--0.04\,s with no
failed requests. Killing an EPaxos replica produced a gap of
0.02--0.09\,s with 10--106 failed requests.

All runs completed successfully according to the experiment harness; the
reported failed requests correspond to requests issued during the
transition and are distinct from run-level failure.

The longer availability gap following Raft leader termination coincided
with the configured Raft election and request-retry behaviour. In contrast,
the tested follower-failure case did not require replacement of the Raft
leader, while the EPaxos replica-failure case continued with the remaining
replicas. These results describe the tested failure scenarios and should
not be interpreted as representative of all leader-based or leaderless
implementations.

The election experiment provides a more controlled characterisation of
Raft-specific recovery under repeated election disruption. The measured
number of failed elections ranged from 0 to 13 across the 60 runs. The
availability gap was strongly associated with the measured election count
($r=0.90$, $p<10^{-4}$), but this relationship is partly mechanical
because the isolation interval itself increases with the configured
disruption duration.

To examine recovery independently of the imposed isolation interval, the
analysis also measures the time from the end of the isolation window to the
first successful request. This post-isolation recovery interval was more
variable and showed a weaker positive association with the measured failed
election count ($r=0.41$, $p=0.001$, $n=60$). This result is reported as an
observed association rather than as evidence of a causal relationship.

\subsection{Correctness Validation}

The correctness harness described in Section~\ref{sec:setup} checks state
convergence under the tested workloads and failure scenarios. All 18 tests
passed (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}): every replied request was applied
exactly once on every replica, the surviving replicas agreed on the final
state, the restarted Raft replicas recovered the committed state from their

normally. Because the correctness workload assigns a unique key to each
request, this is primarily a state-convergence check rather than an

\subsection{Communication Cost}

The communication-cost family evaluates sensitivity to additional
application-level one-way message delay and should be interpreted as a
controlled communication-condition experiment rather than a direct
simulation of a physical network.

Figure~\ref{fig:delay-merged}a shows mean throughput at each delay
level with zero jitter for four implementations. The implementations
exhibited substantially different sensitivity to the injected delay.
HashiCorp Raft decreased from 4{,}502\,req/s at zero delay to
1{,}280\,req/s at 10\,ms, a 72\,\% reduction. nvb EPaxos decreased from
4{,}346 to 833\,req/s, an 81\,\% reduction, while the original EPaxos
implementation decreased from 5{,}527 to 221\,req/s, a 96\,\% reduction.
The etcd Raft implementation showed a different response: its throughput
decreased from 6{,}420\,req/s at zero delay to 375\,req/s at 1\,ms and
45\,req/s at 10\,ms.

The measured ordering therefore differed across implementations under
added communication delay. The 95\,\% confidence intervals of the means
were separated between the evaluated implementations at every non-zero
delay level.

Median latency increased with the injected delay for all four
implementations. At 10\,ms, the measured p50 values were 22.6\,ms for
HashiCorp Raft, 36.2\,ms for nvb EPaxos, 125.9\,ms for original EPaxos,
and 703.4\,ms for etcd Raft. The implementation ordering observed for
latency at this condition corresponded to the throughput ordering.

The jitter sweep produced a smaller change than the corresponding increase
in fixed delay. At a 5\,ms base delay, increasing jitter from 0 to 100\,\%
reduced throughput from 2{,}316 to 1{,}760\,req/s for HashiCorp Raft
(24\,\%), from 1{,}432 to 1{,}082\,req/s for nvb EPaxos (24\,\%), from 479
to 305\,req/s for original EPaxos (36\,\%), and from 87 to 59\,req/s for
etcd Raft (32\,\%).

Under the implemented jitter model, 100\,\% jitter also raises the mean
injected delay from 5 to 7.5\,ms, so the experiment cannot separate delay
variance from that increase in mean cost.

\subsection{Persistence Matching}

\label{sec:persistence-results}

The recorded baseline compares Raft implementations that persist their logs to
storage with EPaxos implementations that keep replica state in memory
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}). The persistence-matching family holds workload,
replica count, concurrency and network constant and varies only the storage
mode, using only modes the implementation itself provides. It is analysed from
its own results subtree and never pooled with the recorded dataset; 140 of its
147 runs were included, and every condition retains its planned ten
repetitions after the replacements described at the end of
Section~\ref{sec:conflict-results}.

Table~\ref{tab:persistence} and
Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-persistence} show throughput at three and five
replicas. The two modes that reproduce the recorded baseline agree with it: at
three replicas the durable HashiCorp Raft median of 4{,}425\,req/s is
1.7\,\% above the recorded 4{,}350\,req/s, and the in-memory original EPaxos
median of 5{,}585\,req/s is 5.6\,\% above the recorded 5{,}291\,req/s, the
upper edge of the 2--3\,\% cross-family spread reported above.

Matching the storage mode changes the direction of the comparison at both
replica counts. With both implementations in memory, HashiCorp Raft measured
29{,}660\,req/s against 5{,}585\,req/s for the original EPaxos (5.3$\times$);
with both durable, 4{,}425 against 2{,}005 (2.2$\times$). In the second pair,
etcd Raft measured 12{,}884\,req/s against 4{,}226\,req/s for nvb EPaxos in
memory at three replicas (3.0$\times$) and 8{,}437 against 1{,}600 at five
replicas (5.3$\times$). Every matched comparison in this family therefore
places the Raft implementation above the EPaxos implementation, and each is
significant (exact Mann--Whitney $U$ test, $p<0.001$). The durable cell of the
second pair has no EPaxos counterpart: the nvb EPaxos library offers only
in-memory storage, and no persistence mechanism was added to fill the gap, so
the figure shows that bar for Raft alone.

The storage modes account for the reversal. Relative to the same
implementation's in-memory median, durable storage retained 15\,\% for
HashiCorp Raft, 48\,\% for etcd Raft and 36\,\% for the original EPaxos at
three replicas, and 13\,\%, 69\,\% and 33\,\% at five replicas ($p<0.001$ for
each comparison). The recorded comparison therefore measured the
implementation with the largest durability cost (HashiCorp Raft, which loses
85\,\% of its in-memory throughput when its log is persisted) against one
whose evaluated mode is in-memory (the original EPaxos, which loses 64\,\%
when made durable). The recorded EPaxos/Raft ratio of 1.22 is consistent with
that pairing, so it should not be read as a property of the two protocol
families. This family measures one workload at two replica counts, so it
establishes that the recorded ordering depends on the storage modes; it does
not establish that persistence-matched deployments would order the two
families the same way at other replica counts or write ratios.

The p50 ordering follows the throughput ordering. At three and five replicas,
durable HashiCorp Raft measured 6.8 and 9.3\,ms against 0.8 and 0.9\,ms in
memory; durable etcd Raft 4.9 and 5.1\,ms against 1.7 and 2.9\,ms in memory;
durable original EPaxos 14.5 and 15.6\,ms against 5.7 and 5.5\,ms in memory;
and in-memory nvb EPaxos 7.1 and 18.6\,ms.

\subsection{Controlled Network Delay}

\label{sec:delay-results}

The communication-cost family described above injects delay inside each
implementation's send path. The controlled network-delay family applies the
same nominal condition from outside the implementations: the runner installs a
\texttt{tc/netem} qdisc inside each replica's network namespace and delays only
traffic addressed to another replica, so no adapter sleeps and no consensus
source is modified. The 0\,ms point is the family's own baseline, recorded
without a qdisc, and all 200 runs were included.

Median throughput declined monotonically with the applied delay for all four
implementations (Figure~\ref{fig:delay-merged}b); a Kruskal--Wallis
test over the five delay levels gives $H=47.06$ against $\chi^2_4=9.49$ for
each implementation, and the observed range of the ten repetitions does not
overlap between adjacent delay levels for any of them. At 10\,ms the
implementations retained 18.1\,\% (HashiCorp Raft), 17.6\,\% (etcd Raft),
13.8\,\% (original EPaxos) and 9.0\,\% (nvb EPaxos) of their 0\,ms medians.
The p50 latency at 10\,ms was 36.9, 25.7, 44.6 and 83.0\,ms, respectively,
against 6.6, 4.7, 5.7 and 6.8\,ms at 0\,ms.

The controlled condition compresses the differences between implementations
that the application-level family measured: the fraction of 0\,ms throughput
retained at the same nominal 10\,ms spans 9.0--18.1\,\% across the four
implementations, a factor of two, where the application-level family spanned
0.7--28.4\,\%, a factor of forty. The near-collapse of etcd Raft measured by
the application-level family is therefore attributable to the injection
mechanism, the per-message sleeps on the adapters' send paths, rather than to
added delay as such, as Section~\ref{sec:threats} anticipated. The ordering
also changes: the two implementations that retained the most under
application-level delay (HashiCorp Raft at 28.4\,\% and nvb EPaxos at
19.2\,\%) retain less under OS-level emulation (18.1\,\% and 9.0\,\%),
consistent with the emulation delaying each inter-replica exchange in both
directions rather than a single send. The two mechanisms are not
interchangeable; the controlled family is the evidence used here for
delay sensitivity.

\subsection{Conflict Validation}

\label{sec:conflict-results}

The recorded conflict-ratio sweep configures how often the workload targets a
hot key, but a configured fraction does not by itself establish the access
pattern that was realised or the protocol-level contention it produced. The
conflict-validation family re-runs the sweep while varying both the configured
hot-key fraction and the number of distinct hot keys that fraction targets,
and records the realised fraction from the per-request hot flag; 480 of its 492
runs were included, twelve excluded as three flagged attempts and their nine
replaced predecessors, so all 48 conditions contain ten repetitions.

Figure~\ref{fig:conflict-validation}(a) shows that the workload delivered what
it configured. With a single hot key the top key's share of requests equalled
the configured fraction (9.9, 24.9, 49.7, 74.8 and 100.0\,\% at 10, 25, 50, 75
and 100\,\%); with eight hot keys it was one eighth of it, as the design
intends. At 100\,\% the workload contains no cold keys at all, leaving one or
eight distinct keys observed per run, so that point is not on the same
continuum as the rest of the sweep and is reported separately below.

Throughput showed no systematic relationship with the configured fraction.
For HashiCorp Raft the medians span 4{,}354 to 4{,}541\,req/s across both
key-set sizes, for etcd Raft 5{,}848 to 6{,}301\,req/s, and for the original
EPaxos 5{,}448 to 5{,}517\,req/s. Kruskal--Wallis tests over the 0--75\,\%
points give $H\leq6.73$ against $\chi^2_4=9.49$ for the six combinations of
these three implementations, and the p50 medians remain in 6.7--6.9\,ms,
4.8--5.1\,ms and 5.66--5.68\,ms respectively. This reproduces the recorded
null result for these implementations and shows that it is not explained
by the configured fraction failing to produce the intended access pattern.
The tails behave the same way over 0--75\,\%: the p99 medians stay within
10.4--11.4\,ms for etcd Raft, 14.8--17.3\,ms for HashiCorp Raft and
13.3--17.0\,ms for the original EPaxos, whereas nvb EPaxos moves from 16.6
to 36.1\,ms together with its median (2.04$\times$ and 2.12$\times$ at
75\,\% against 0\,\%).

nvb EPaxos is the exception. Its median throughput declined monotonically with
the configured fraction over 0--75\,\%, from 4{,}123 to 2{,}129\,req/s with one
hot key and from 4{,}238 to 2{,}324\,req/s with eight ($H=42.28$ and
$H=41.84$), and its p50 rose from 7.3 to 13.9\,ms and from 7.1 to
12.4\,ms. At 100\,\% both of its medians rise above their 0\,\% values, to
6{,}103\,req/s and 6{,}631\,req/s with p50 values of 5.1 and 4.5\,ms. The
reversal coincides with the degenerate keyspace of that point; the
instrumentation does not establish its mechanism, and this implementation
exposes no protocol counters with which to probe it further. Conflict
sensitivity in this family is therefore implementation-specific: three of the
four implementations show no throughput response to the configured fraction
over the range in which the keyspace still contains cold keys, while the
fourth declines monotonically.

Figure~\ref{fig:conflict-validation}(b) reports the one protocol-level
indicator available: the slow-path share of the original EPaxos
implementation declines as the configured fraction rises, from 97.8 to
43.8\,\% with one hot key and from 97.9 to 72.5\,\% with eight. The
\texttt{conflicted} counter moves with it, but in this implementation it
counts replicas whose \texttt{PreAccept} replies disagree on an instance's
sequence number and dependencies, not key conflicts. Both counters are reset
whenever a client connection is accepted, so they cover about 9.5\,\% of the
successful requests rather than the measured phase; that coverage is
near-constant across the conditions of this family, which makes the shares
comparable between them but leaves them a within-window indicator rather than
a census. Read with that limitation, the direction is the opposite of the
expectation that a higher configured conflict fraction forces more slow-path
executions. It is consistent with instances on a hotter key being serialised
onto that key's dependency chain, where replicas agree more often; the
available instrumentation does not separate that reading from others, and no
counter is available for nvb EPaxos.

Two replacement rules were applied to the three families, both keeping the
original attempt in the raw dataset. An attempt that failed, or that host
telemetry flagged as contaminated, was re-run as a new attempt. A repetition
that is anomalous without being flagged is invisible to that rule, so a second
rule was fixed in advance and applied uniformly to every condition: an included
repetition measuring below 0.7 times the median of its own configuration is
re-run, and the report keeps the replacement whatever its value. Eleven
repetitions met the second rule and seven attempts were replaced by the first.
No included repetition of the three families now measures below 0.7 times its
condition's median, no repetition's p50 exceeds 1.5 times that of its
condition, and every condition has its planned ten observations; replacing
those repetitions moved no median reported in this section by more than
3\,\%.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The measurements support the following observations under the evaluated
conditions:

\begin{itemize}

\item Throughput decreased with replica count for both implementations, but
the EPaxos/Raft ratio changed with cluster size, and the difference was
statistically detectable only at three and five replicas.

\item Eight of 1{,}302 repetitions stalled (two Raft, six EPaxos),
motivating the use of medians. EPaxos at seven and nine replicas also showed
request timeouts, which account for part of its reduction there through the
closed-loop concurrency accounting of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\item Client concurrency widened the gap between the implementations rather
than narrowing it: the EPaxos advantage was largest at the highest
concurrency tested, while at the lowest the latency ordering of the two
reversed.

\item Write ratio and conflict ratio produced no systematic throughput trend
(Kruskal--Wallis $H\leq4.44$ against critical values of 9.49 and 12.59).
Conflict validation showed that the configured fraction was realised exactly
in the workload and that three of the four implementations still showed no
response, while nvb EPaxos declined monotonically: conflict sensitivity is
implementation-specific.

\item Failure of the Raft leader produced an availability gap two orders of
magnitude larger than failure of a Raft follower or an EPaxos replica.

\item The elected leader was the CPU hotspot in the evaluated HashiCorp Raft
implementation, using 2.2 times a follower's CPU at three replicas and 3.8
times at nine, while the two protocol families used similar per-replica CPU
overall.

\item Implementation choices change the measured relationship. The spread of
retained throughput differs by about a factor of twenty between the two delay
mechanisms (9.0--18.1\,\% against 0.7--28.4\,\%); matching the storage mode
reverses the recorded ordering at both replica counts tested; and the
recorded EPaxos advantage at three replicas reflects which storage mode each
implementation was run in.

\end{itemize}
