\section{Evaluation}

\label{sec:evaluation}

Unless otherwise stated, reported point estimates are medians over ten
independent repetitions. Unless otherwise specified, results use three
replicas, 100\,\% writes, and client concurrency 32. All figures are
generated from the processed benchmark data by \texttt{lab/report/figures.py}.
Figures show medians across repetitions, with individual repetition values
overlaid where available.

\subsection{Throughput and Scalability}

Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-scaling} shows measured throughput as the
number of replicas increases. Throughput decreased with replica count for
both evaluated implementations. At three replicas, the measured median was
5{,}292\,req/s for EPaxos and 4{,}351\,req/s for Raft. At five replicas,
the medians were 5{,}443 and 3{,}150\,req/s, respectively, while at nine
replicas they were 2{,}094 and 2{,}113\,req/s (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}).
Relative to the three-replica measurement within this experiment family,
the nine-replica median was 60\,\% lower for EPaxos and 51\,\% lower for
Raft. The ratio of the two medians was 1.22 at three replicas, 1.73 at
five, 1.18 at seven, and 0.99 at nine replicas. Thus, the measured
throughput difference varied with replica count, and no statistically
significant difference was detected at nine replicas.

We report medians rather than means because a small number of repetitions
stalled. Across the entire dataset (1{,}302 runs), eight repetitions
completed fewer than 10\,\% of the requests of their configuration's
median: two Raft runs (0.3\,\% of Raft runs) and six EPaxos runs (1.0\,\%
of EPaxos runs). Seven of the eight were slow from start to finish with no
failed requests, while one EPaxos run at nine replicas showed persistent
request timeouts. The two Raft stalls coincided with elevated host load
(load average 6.9--8.5 against 3--4 for healthy runs of the same
configuration). The six EPaxos stalls occurred at both low and high host
load. None of the eight runs was flagged by the host-telemetry
contamination rule, so they remain in the dataset. The means are therefore
lower than the medians for both implementations, and the mean-based
EPaxos/Raft ratio (1.29--1.75) is larger than the corresponding median
ratio at seven and nine replicas. The observed stall counts (2 of 690
Raft runs and 6 of 612 EPaxos runs) were not significantly different
(Fisher's exact test, $p=0.16$).

A second anomaly affects EPaxos at seven and nine replicas: request
timeouts. Every EPaxos run at those sizes except one had 30--250 requests
time out at the 2{,}000\,ms client timeout, corresponding to success rates
of 98.6--99.9\,\%, while Raft runs at the same sizes had no timeouts. Each
timeout occupies a client worker for the full 2\,s, reducing the
concurrency available to successful requests. For a closed-loop client,
Little's law can be applied to the successful requests and timed-out
requests separately. In particular,

$$
N \approx X_{\mathrm{ok}}W_{\mathrm{ok}}
  + X_{\mathrm{timeout}}W_{\mathrm{timeout}},
$$

where $X_{\mathrm{timeout}}=N_{\mathrm{timeout}}/20,\mathrm{s}$ and
$W_{\mathrm{timeout}}=2,\mathrm{s}$. This accounting is consistent with
every EPaxos run at seven and nine replicas. The successful requests
themselves completed in 5.4--5.6\,ms at p50, similar to the corresponding
three-replica measurements. Thus, the reduced measured throughput is
accounted for by requests occupying workers until the timeout rather than
by an increase in the p50 latency of successful requests. The timeout
pattern is observed only in the seven- and nine-replica EPaxos
configurations; the available instrumentation does not establish the
specific execution-path mechanism responsible for it.

The same nominal configuration (three replicas, 100\,\% writes, and
concurrency 32) also occurs independently in several other steady-state
experiment families. These repeated measurements provide an indication of
run-to-run variability. The four family medians were 4{,}351, 4{,}368,
4{,}483, and 4{,}441\,req/s for Raft, and 5{,}292, 5{,}288, 5{,}177, and
5{,}250\,req/s for EPaxos. The EPaxos medians differed by approximately
2\,\% across these measurements, and the Raft medians by approximately
3\,\%. Cross-family variability was therefore of similar magnitude for the
two implementations.

For the scaling family, the difference between the implementations was
statistically significant at three and five replicas (exact Mann--Whitney
$U$ test, $p<0.001$), while no statistically significant difference was
detected at seven ($p=0.12$) or nine ($p=0.32$) replicas. The 95\,\%
bootstrap confidence intervals of the medians overlap at seven and nine
replicas (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The measured EPaxos/Raft throughput
difference was therefore statistically significant only at the smaller
cluster sizes in this experiment.

\subsection{Latency}

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-replica-scaling} shows median (p50) latency as the
number of replicas increases. At three replicas, the median latency was
6.9\,ms for Raft and 5.6\,ms for EPaxos. Raft latency increased to
9.2\,ms at five replicas and 12.0\,ms at seven replicas, reaching
14.6\,ms at nine replicas. EPaxos remained within a relatively narrow
range of 5.4--5.6\,ms across the tested replica counts
(Table~\ref{tab:latency}).

The evaluated Raft implementation therefore showed a larger change in
median latency with replica count than the evaluated EPaxos implementation
under this workload. The Raft p50 increased monotonically with replica
count, whereas the EPaxos p50 remained within the 5.4--5.6\,ms range.

The p50 values should be read together with the tail. The p99 was 21.4\,ms
for Raft and 23.8\,ms for EPaxos at three replicas, and increased to
26--29\,ms for Raft at seven to nine replicas while remaining at
14--16\,ms for EPaxos. The median of the per-repetition p50 values reduces
the influence of the stalled repetitions described earlier.

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-concurrency} shows median latency across client
concurrency. At concurrency 1, median latency was 2.8\,ms for Raft and
5.5\,ms for EPaxos. At concurrency 32, the corresponding values were
6.9\,ms and 5.6\,ms. At concurrency 256, they were 12.1\,ms and
5.6\,ms.

The relative latency ordering therefore changed with concurrency. Raft had
the lower median at concurrency 1, while EPaxos had the lower median at
concurrency 32 and above. At concurrency 256, the measured p50 was
5.6\,ms for EPaxos and 12.1\,ms for Raft.

As with throughput, the same nominal configuration (three replicas,
concurrency 32) is measured by several experiment families. With medians,
the p50 was consistent across families: Raft measured 6.9\,ms in the
scaling family and 6.9\,ms in the concurrency family, while EPaxos
measured 5.6\,ms in both.

For a closed-loop client, Little's law relates mean latency to concurrency
and throughput as $W=N/X$. The reported p50 is the median rather than the
mean, so it need not equal this quantity. At concurrency 256, the Raft p50
of 12.1\,ms was close to $N/X$ (256/19{,}333 $\approx$ 13.2\,ms), while the
EPaxos p50 of 5.6\,ms was below $N/X$ (256/41{,}077 $\approx$ 6.2\,ms).
This difference is compatible with a response-time distribution whose
median is below its mean. We report p50, p95, and p99 in the processed
dataset and use p50 here as the central-tendency summary.

At seven and nine replicas, the EPaxos p50 of 5.4--5.5\,ms was substantially
below $N/X$ (32/2{,}094--3{,}053 $\approx$ 10.5--15.3\,ms). The difference
is accounted for by the request timeouts described above. Every EPaxos run
at these sizes except one had requests time out at the 2{,}000\,ms client
timeout, and each timeout occupies a worker for the full 2\,s. For a
closed-loop client with concurrency $N$, the corresponding accounting is

$$
N \approx X_{\mathrm{ok}}W_{\mathrm{ok}}
  + X_{\mathrm{timeout}}W_{\mathrm{timeout}},
$$

where $X_{\mathrm{timeout}}=N_{\mathrm{timeout}}/20,\mathrm{s}$ and
$W_{\mathrm{timeout}}=2,\mathrm{s}$. This accounting is consistent with
every EPaxos run at seven and nine replicas. The low throughput can
therefore be accounted for without requiring an increase in the p50
latency of successful requests. Raft runs at the same sizes had no
timeouts, and their measured $N/X$ values were closer to their reported
p50 latencies.

\subsection{Resource Utilisation}

Figure~\ref{fig:resource-utilisation} summarises the per-replica resource
measurements. Across the evaluated runs, mean per-replica CPU utilisation
was 35.8\,\% for Raft and 35.0\,\% for EPaxos. Mean peak per-replica RSS
was 26.4\,MB for Raft and 46.8\,MB for EPaxos. Mean per-replica network
rates were 441\,kB/s RX and 443\,kB/s TX for Raft, compared with
408\,kB/s RX and 392\,kB/s TX for EPaxos.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/resource-utilisation.pdf}
\caption{Mean per-replica CPU utilisation, peak per-replica RSS, and
mean per-replica network rates, aggregated over the evaluated runs.}
\label{fig:resource-utilisation}

Figure~\ref{fig:per-replica-cpu} examines the distribution of CPU
utilisation in the nine-replica scaling experiment. Raft per-replica means
ranged from 16.3\,\% to 29.4\,\%. The replica serving as leader during a
given repetition generally consumed less CPU than the followers. Labelling
each run by the leader reported at the start of its measured phase, the
leader/follower CPU ratio was 0.61--0.64 at three replicas, 0.70 at five
replicas, and 0.73--0.75 at seven and nine replicas, using the medians over
the ten repetitions.

This pattern is consistent with the request path of the evaluated
HashiCorp Raft implementation, in which the leader sends AppendEntries and
waits for quorum responses while followers receive, persist, and apply
replicated log entries. The measurements do not isolate the contributions
of persistence, state-machine application, or communication processing to
the observed CPU difference. They therefore establish that the leader did
not form the CPU hotspot in the evaluated implementation, but do not by
themselves identify the individual processing components responsible for
the difference.

\subsection{Failure Recovery Behaviour}

Failure outcomes differed by failure type (Table~\ref{tab:failure},
Figure~\ref{fig:failure-recovery}). Killing the Raft leader produced a
write-availability gap of 1.81--4.74\,s and 2{,}169--4{,}133 failed
requests. Killing a Raft follower produced a gap of 0.02--0.04\,s with no
failed requests. Killing an EPaxos replica produced a gap of
0.02--0.09\,s with 10--106 failed requests.

All runs completed successfully according to the experiment harness. The
reported failed requests correspond to requests issued during the
transition and are therefore distinct from run-level failure.

The longer availability gap following Raft leader termination coincided
with the configured Raft election and request-retry behaviour. In contrast,
the tested follower-failure case did not require replacement of the Raft
leader, while the EPaxos replica-failure case continued with the remaining
replicas. These results describe the tested failure scenarios and should
not be interpreted as representative of all leader-based or leaderless
implementations.

The election experiment provides a more controlled characterisation of
Raft-specific recovery under repeated election disruption. The measured
number of failed elections ranged from 0 to 13 across the 60 runs. The
availability gap was strongly associated with the measured election count
($r=0.90$, $p<10^{-4}$), but this relationship is partly mechanical
because the isolation interval itself increases with the configured
disruption duration.

To examine recovery independently of the imposed isolation interval, the
analysis also measures the time from the end of the isolation window to the
first successful request. This post-isolation recovery interval was more
variable and showed a weaker positive association with the measured failed
election count ($r=0.41$, $p=0.001$, $n=60$). This result is reported as an
observed association rather than as evidence of a causal relationship.

\subsection{Correctness Validation}

The performance experiments are not intended to constitute a formal
verification of consensus correctness. The separate correctness harness
described in Section~\ref{sec:setup} instead checks state convergence under
the tested workloads and failure scenarios.

The harness issued 2{,}000 deterministic requests per test. Each request
used a unique request-derived key and deterministic value, allowing the
expected state to be reconstructed independently. After each test, the
harness collected the state-machine snapshot from each available replica
through the read-only administration RPC and compared the resulting states
without relying on internal log representations.

The harness tested concurrency levels 1, 8, and 32 and the failure scenarios
used by the failure experiments. Raft leader and follower replicas were
killed and restarted, while an EPaxos replica was likewise killed and
restarted. For Raft, the restarted replica was required to recover the
committed state from its persistent log. For EPaxos, the evaluated
in-memory configuration was required to resume participation after restart;
persistence across restart was not part of this validation.

All 18 correctness tests passed (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}). The tests
therefore provide evidence that the evaluated implementations converged to
the expected state for the tested request and failure scenarios and resumed
processing after the injected faults.

This validation does not establish general linearizability, exhaustive
ordering correctness, or correctness under workloads outside the tested
cases. In particular, because the correctness workload assigns a unique
key to each request, it primarily tests state convergence rather than
providing an independent verification of operation-order semantics.

\subsection{Communication Cost}

The communication-cost family evaluates sensitivity to additional
application-level one-way message delay. It should therefore be interpreted
as a controlled communication-condition experiment rather than as a direct
simulation of a physical network.

Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-commcost} shows mean throughput at each delay
level with zero jitter for four implementations. The implementations
exhibited substantially different sensitivity to the injected delay.
HashiCorp Raft decreased from 4{,}502\,req/s at zero delay to
1{,}280\,req/s at 10\,ms, a 72\,\% reduction. nvb EPaxos decreased from
4{,}346 to 833\,req/s, an 81\,\% reduction, while the original EPaxos
implementation decreased from 5{,}527 to 221\,req/s, a 96\,\% reduction.
The etcd Raft implementation showed a different response: its throughput
decreased from 6{,}420\,req/s at zero delay to 375\,req/s at 1\,ms and
45\,req/s at 10\,ms.

The measured ordering therefore differed across implementations under
added communication delay. The 95\,\% confidence intervals of the means
were separated between the evaluated implementations at every non-zero
delay level.

Median latency increased with the injected delay for all four
implementations. At 10\,ms, the measured p50 values were 22.6\,ms for

and 703.4\,ms for etcd Raft (Figure~\ref{fig:latency-commcost}). The
implementation ordering observed for latency at this condition corresponded
to the throughput ordering.

The jitter sweep produced a smaller change than the corresponding increase
in fixed delay. At a 5\,ms base delay, increasing jitter from 0 to 100\,\%
reduced throughput from 2{,}316 to 1{,}760\,req/s for HashiCorp Raft
(24\,\%), from 1{,}432 to 1{,}082\,req/s for nvb EPaxos (24\,\%), from 479
to 305\,req/s for original EPaxos (36\,\%), and from 87 to 59\,req/s for
etcd Raft (32\,\%).

Under the implemented jitter model, 100\,\% jitter increases the maximum
additional delay from zero to 5\,ms and increases the mean injected delay
from 5 to 7.5\,ms. The measured throughput changes are therefore consistent
with an increase in average injected delay. The experiment does not
separate the effect of delay variance from the corresponding change in mean
delay.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The measurements support the following observations under the evaluated
conditions:

\begin{itemize}

\item Throughput decreased as replica count increased for both evaluated
implementations. In the scaling experiment, the nine-replica median was
60\,\% below the three-replica median for EPaxos and 51\,\% below it for
Raft.

\item The relative throughput difference between the evaluated Raft and
EPaxos implementations varied with replica count. The EPaxos/Raft median
ratio was 1.22 at three replicas, 1.73 at five, 1.18 at seven, and 0.99 at
nine, where no statistically significant difference was detected.

\item Eight repetitions across the dataset stalled (two Raft and six
EPaxos), motivating the use of medians for the reported point estimates.
The Raft stalls coincided with elevated host load, whereas the EPaxos
stalls occurred at both low and high host load. The mean-based EPaxos/Raft
ratio was 1.29--1.75 and was larger than the corresponding median ratio at
seven and nine replicas.

\item Throughput continued to increase with client concurrency up to the
tested maximum of 256. At that point, the measured medians were
41{,}077\,req/s for EPaxos and 19{,}333\,req/s for Raft.

\item Median latency depended on both concurrency and replica count. EPaxos
had the lower measured median at the baseline configuration and throughout
the replica-scaling measurements. At concurrency 256, the p50 was
5.6\,ms for EPaxos compared with 12.1\,ms for Raft.

\item Write ratio and conflict ratio did not produce a systematic throughput
trend over the tested ranges. Kruskal--Wallis tests of the write-ratio and
conflict-ratio groups were not significant for either implementation
($H\leq3.71$ against critical values of 9.49 and 12.59).

\item Raft leader termination produced a longer availability gap than the
tested Raft follower and EPaxos replica termination scenarios. The
additional election experiment showed an observed association between
failed-election count and recovery intervals, although the relationship
was not interpreted causally.

\item Per-replica CPU utilisation was distributed differently in the
nine-replica scaling experiment. Across the scaling configurations, the
measured leader/follower CPU ratio remained below 1.0 (0.61--0.64 at
three replicas, 0.70 at five, and 0.73--0.75 at seven and nine). Thus, the
leader did not form the CPU hotspot in the evaluated HashiCorp Raft
implementation. These measurements do not by themselves identify CPU
saturation as a throughput bottleneck.

\item Communication sensitivity differed substantially across the four
evaluated implementations. At 10\,ms of application-level one-way delay,
throughput reductions ranged from 72\,\% for HashiCorp Raft to 96\,\% for
the original EPaxos implementation, while etcd Raft showed a much steeper
decline beginning at the smallest non-zero delay tested.

\item The communication-cost results show that implementation choice can
materially change the measured performance relationship between
leader-based and leaderless consensus. Results from a single implementation
of each protocol should therefore be interpreted as measurements of those
implementations rather than as implementation-independent properties of
the corresponding protocol families.

\end{itemize}
