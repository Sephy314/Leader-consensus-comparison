\section{Evaluation}

\label{sec:evaluation}

All reported values are means over ten independent repetitions unless
otherwise stated. Unless otherwise specified, results use three replicas,
100\,\% writes, and client concurrency 32. All figures are generated from
the processed benchmark data by \texttt{lab/report/figures.py}. Figures
show means across repetitions, with individual repetition values overlaid
where available.

\subsection{Throughput and Scalability}

Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-scaling} shows measured mean throughput as the
number of replicas increases. Throughput decreased with replica count for
both evaluated implementations. At three replicas, the measured mean was
5{,}330\,req/s for EPaxos and 3{,}531\,req/s for Raft. At five replicas,
the means were 4{,}969 and 2{,}841\,req/s, respectively, while at nine
replicas they were 2{,}283 and 1{,}768\,req/s (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}).

Relative to the three-replica measurement within this experiment family,
the nine-replica mean was 57\,\% lower for EPaxos and 50\,\% lower for
Raft. The ratio of the two means was 1.51 at three replicas, 1.75 at five,
1.36 at seven, and 1.29 at nine replicas. Thus, the measured throughput
difference varied with replica count rather than remaining at a constant
ratio.

The same nominal configuration (three replicas, 100\,\% writes, and
concurrency 32) also occurs independently in several other steady-state
experiment families. These repeated measurements provide an indication of
run-to-run variability. The four family means were 4{,}282, 4{,}006,
4{,}221, and 3{,}531\,req/s for Raft, and 4{,}926, 5{,}011, 5{,}147, and
5{,}330\,req/s for EPaxos. The EPaxos means differed by approximately 8\,\%
across these measurements, whereas the Raft means differed by approximately
21\,\%. The scaling-family Raft result was the lowest of these measurements
and also had a relatively large standard deviation (1{,}453\,req/s).
Consequently, differences between individual experiment families should be
interpreted together with the observed run-to-run variability rather than
as independent protocol effects.

\subsection{Latency}

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-replica-scaling} shows median (p50) latency as the
number of replicas increases. At three replicas, the median latency was
12.3\,ms for Raft and 5.6\,ms for EPaxos. Raft latency increased to
16.8\,ms at five replicas and 25.5\,ms at seven replicas before decreasing
to 22.8\,ms at nine replicas. EPaxos remained within a relatively narrow
range of 5.6--6.2\,ms across the tested replica counts
(Table~\ref{tab:latency}).

The results therefore show a substantially larger change in median latency
with replica count for the evaluated Raft implementation than for the
evaluated EPaxos implementation under this workload. The non-monotonic
nine-replica Raft result also indicates that the relationship is not simply
a linear function of replica count.

The p50 values should be read together with the tail. The p99 was 42.0\,ms
for Raft and 21.2\,ms for EPaxos at three replicas, and grew to 64--74\,ms
for Raft at seven to nine replicas. For EPaxos, the p99 at seven and nine
replicas (221.9 and 213.7\,ms) is dominated by a single repetition that
completed only 338 requests in the 20\,s phase (against 27{,}000--115{,}000
for the other repetitions) without a host-telemetry anomaly; excluding that
repetition, the EPaxos p99 at those sizes is 14--28\,ms. The tail is
therefore not a stable property of the EPaxos implementation at these
cluster sizes, and we do not interpret it as one.

Figure~\ref{fig:latency-concurrency} shows median latency across client
concurrency. At concurrency 1, median latency was 4.1\,ms for Raft and
5.5\,ms for EPaxos. At concurrency 32, the corresponding values were
7.4\,ms and 5.9\,ms. At concurrency 256, they were 13.4\,ms and
14.0\,ms.

Thus, the relative latency ordering changed with concurrency. Raft had the
lower median at concurrency 1, EPaxos had the lower median around the
baseline concurrency of 32, and the two implementations had similar
medians at concurrency 256. The results therefore do not support a
concurrency-independent latency ordering.

As with throughput, the same nominal configuration (three replicas,
concurrency 32) is measured by several families, and the p50 varies between
them: Raft measured 12.3\,ms in the scaling family and 7.4\,ms in the
concurrency family, while EPaxos measured 5.6 and 5.9\,ms. The
EPaxos/Raft p50 ratio at this configuration is therefore 1.25--2.2 depending
on the family, and a single family should not be quoted in isolation.

For a closed-loop client, Little's law relates the mean latency to the
concurrency and throughput ($W = N/X$). The reported p50 is the median, not
the mean, so it can lie below or above this value. At concurrency 256, the
EPaxos p50 (14.0\,ms) exceeds $N/X$ (7.5\,ms), which is possible only if the
latency distribution is left-skewed---most requests wait in the queue while
a minority complete quickly---and indicates that the median is not a good
summary of the response-time distribution at high concurrency. We therefore
report p50, p95, and p99 in the processed dataset and use p50 here only as
the central-tendency summary.

\subsection{Resource Utilisation}

Figure~\ref{fig:resource-utilisation} summarises the per-replica resource
measurements. Across the evaluated runs, mean per-replica CPU utilisation
was 35.8\,\% for Raft and 35.0\,\% for EPaxos. Mean peak per-replica RSS
was 26.4\,MB for Raft and 46.8\,MB for EPaxos. Mean per-replica network
rates were 441\,kB/s RX and 443\,kB/s TX for Raft, compared with
408\,kB/s RX and 392\,kB/s TX for EPaxos.

\centering

\caption{Mean per-replica CPU utilisation, peak per-replica RSS, and

Figure~\ref{fig:per-replica-cpu} examines the distribution of CPU
utilisation in the nine-replica scaling experiment. Raft per-replica means
ranged from 16.3\,\% to 29.4\,\%. The replica serving as leader during a
given repetition generally consumed more CPU than the followers, and
leadership rotated across repetitions. Consequently, the aggregate
per-replica means reflect both the role of each replica and the fraction of
repetitions in which it was leader.

EPaxos per-replica means ranged from 22.4\,\% to 23.0\,\%, with no comparable
single-replica concentration. These measurements are consistent with a more
even distribution of CPU utilisation across replicas in the evaluated
EPaxos implementation. They do not, however, establish CPU saturation as
the mechanism limiting either implementation's throughput.

\subsection{Failure Recovery Behaviour}

Failure outcomes differed by failure type (Table~\ref{tab:failure},
Figure~\ref{fig:failure-recovery}). Killing the Raft leader produced a
write-availability gap of 1.81--4.74\,s and 2{,}169--4{,}133 failed
requests. Killing a Raft follower produced a gap of 0.02--0.04\,s with no
failed requests. Killing an EPaxos replica produced a gap of
0.02--0.09\,s with 10--106 failed requests.

All runs completed successfully according to the experiment harness. The
reported failed requests correspond to requests issued during the
transition and are therefore distinct from a run-level failure.

The substantially longer gap following Raft leader termination coincides
with the configured Raft election and request-retry behaviour. In contrast,
the tested follower-failure case did not require replacing the Raft leader,
and the EPaxos replica-failure case continued with the remaining replicas.
These results describe the tested failure scenarios; they do not imply
that all leader-based or leaderless implementations exhibit the same
failure-recovery behaviour.

The election experiment provides a more controlled characterisation of
Raft-specific recovery under repeated election disruption. The measured
number of failed elections ranged from 0 to 13 across the 60 runs. The
availability gap was strongly associated with the measured election count
($r=0.90$, $p<10^{-4}$), but this relationship is partly mechanical because
the isolation interval itself increases with the configured disruption
duration.

To examine recovery independently of the imposed isolation interval, the
analysis also measures the time from the end of the isolation window to the
first successful request. This post-isolation recovery interval was more
variable and showed a weaker positive association with the measured failed
election count ($r=0.41$, $p=0.001$, $n=60$). This result is reported as an
observed association rather than as evidence of a causal relationship.

\subsection{Correctness Validation}

The performance experiments are not intended to constitute a formal
verification of consensus correctness. The separate correctness harness
described in Section~\ref{sec:setup} instead checks state convergence under
the tested workloads and failure scenarios.

The harness issued 2{,}000 deterministic requests per test. Each request
used a unique request-derived key and deterministic value, allowing the
expected state to be reconstructed independently. After each test, the
harness collected the state-machine snapshot from each available replica
through the read-only administration RPC and compared the resulting states
without relying on internal log representations.

The harness tested concurrency levels 1, 8, and 32 and the failure scenarios
used by the failure experiments. Raft leader and follower replicas were
killed and restarted, while an EPaxos replica was likewise killed and
restarted. For Raft, the restarted replica was required to recover the
committed state from its persistent log. For EPaxos, the evaluated
in-memory configuration was required to resume participation after restart;
persistence across restart was not part of this validation.

All 18 correctness tests passed (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}). The tests
therefore provide evidence that the evaluated implementations converged to
the expected state for the tested request and failure scenarios and resumed
processing after the injected faults.

This validation does not establish general linearizability, exhaustive
ordering correctness, or correctness under workloads outside the tested
cases. In particular, because the correctness workload assigns a unique
key to each request, it is primarily a state-convergence check rather than
an independent verification of operation-order semantics.

\subsection{Communication Cost}

The communication-cost family evaluates sensitivity to additional
application-level one-way message delay. It should therefore be interpreted
as a controlled communication-condition experiment rather than as a direct
simulation of a physical network.

Figure~\ref{fig:throughput-commcost} shows mean throughput at each delay
level with zero jitter for four implementations. The implementations
exhibited substantially different sensitivity to the injected delay.
HashiCorp Raft decreased from 4{,}502\,req/s at zero delay to
1{,}280\,req/s at 10\,ms, a 72\,\% reduction. nvb EPaxos decreased from
4{,}346 to 833\,req/s, an 81\,\% reduction, while the original EPaxos
implementation decreased from 5{,}527 to 221\,req/s, a 96\,\% reduction.

The etcd Raft implementation showed a different response: its throughput
decreased from 6{,}420\,req/s at zero delay to 375\,req/s at 1\,ms and
45\,req/s at 10\,ms. The resulting ordering therefore differs from the
three-replica baseline comparison: implementation choice changes the
relative throughput observed under added communication delay. The 95\,\%
confidence intervals of the means are separated between the evaluated
implementations at every non-zero delay level.

Median latency increased with the injected delay for all four
implementations. At 10\,ms, the measured p50 values were 22.6\,ms for

and 703.4\,ms for etcd Raft (Figure~\ref{fig:latency-commcost}). The
implementation ordering observed for latency at this condition corresponded
to the throughput ordering.

The jitter sweep produced a smaller change than the corresponding increase
in fixed delay. At a 5\,ms base delay, increasing jitter from 0 to 100\,\%
reduced throughput from 2{,}316 to 1{,}760\,req/s for HashiCorp Raft
(24\,\%), from 1{,}432 to 1{,}082\,req/s for nvb EPaxos (24\,\%), from 479
to 305\,req/s for original EPaxos (36\,\%), and from 87 to 59\,req/s for
etcd Raft (32\,\%).

Under the implemented jitter model, 100\,\% jitter increases the maximum
additional delay from zero to 5\,ms and increases the mean injected delay
from 5 to 7.5\,ms. The measured throughput changes are therefore consistent
with an increase in the average injected delay. The experiment does not
separate the effects of delay variance from the corresponding change in
mean delay.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

The measurements support the following observations under the evaluated
conditions:

\begin{itemize}

\item Throughput decreased as replica count increased for both evaluated
implementations. In the scaling experiment, the nine-replica mean was
57\,\% below the three-replica mean for EPaxos and 50\,\% below it for
Raft.

\item The relative throughput difference between the evaluated Raft and
EPaxos implementations varied with replica count. The EPaxos/Raft mean
ratio ranged from 1.29 to 1.75 across the tested replica counts, rather
than remaining constant.

\item Throughput continued to increase with client concurrency up to the
tested maximum of 256. At that point, the measured means were
33{,}971\,req/s for EPaxos and 17{,}845\,req/s for Raft.

\item Median latency depended on concurrency and replica count. EPaxos had
lower median latency than Raft at the tested baseline and across the
replica-scaling measurements, while the latency ordering changed at other
concurrency levels.

\item Write ratio and conflict ratio did not produce a systematic throughput
trend over the tested ranges. The observed variation was comparable to
run-to-run variation in the corresponding experiment families.

\item Raft leader termination produced a substantially longer availability
gap than the tested Raft follower and EPaxos replica termination scenarios.
The measured election experiment additionally showed an association between
failed-election count and recovery intervals, although the relationship is
reported descriptively rather than causally.

\item Per-replica CPU utilisation was distributed differently in the
nine-replica scaling experiment. Raft showed higher utilisation for the
replica serving as leader during a repetition, whereas EPaxos showed a more
even distribution across replicas. These observations do not by themselves
identify CPU saturation as a throughput bottleneck.

\item Communication sensitivity differed substantially across the four
evaluated implementations. At 10\,ms of application-level one-way delay,
throughput reductions ranged from 72\,\% for HashiCorp Raft to 96\,\% for
the original EPaxos implementation, while etcd Raft showed a much steeper
decline beginning at the smallest non-zero delay tested.

\item The communication-cost results demonstrate that implementation choice
can materially change the measured performance relationship between
leader-based and leaderless consensus. Consequently, results from a single
implementation of each protocol should not be interpreted as
implementation-independent properties of the protocol families.

\end{itemize}
