\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:conclusion}

This study conducted a controlled experimental comparison of Raft and EPaxos as representative leader-based and leaderless consensus designs, varying replica count, client concurrency, workload composition, conflict ratio, failure mode, communication cost, and persistence configuration to distinguish effects associated with consensus structure from those introduced by implementation and experimental environment.

Increasing the replica count reduced throughput in both implementations, while the evaluated EPaxos configuration maintained higher throughput under high client concurrency; a Raft-specific scaling experiment showed this penalty persists as concurrency increases, with CPU utilisation increasingly concentrated at the leader. These observations are consistent with a leader-centred request path, although the experiments do not establish that protocol structure alone accounts for the observed differences. Workload composition produced no uniform response: only nvb EPaxos responded to the configured conflict fraction.

The failure experiments reveal another consequence of centralised request processing: in the tested three-replica configuration, leader failure interrupted write processing until a replacement leader was established, whereas follower failure in Raft and replica failure in EPaxos produced substantially shorter interruptions, characterising the observed failure behaviour rather than establishing protocol-independent recovery bounds.

The implementation-sensitivity experiments provide an important qualification to direct protocol-level comparisons. Implementations belonging to the same consensus family differed in absolute throughput and latency and did not necessarily respond identically to controlled communication delays; implementation-level mechanisms could materially alter the measured results without changing the underlying consensus algorithm. Absolute performance measurements should therefore be interpreted as properties of a particular implementation and environment, rather than as intrinsic performance constants of Raft or EPaxos.

Taken together, the findings do not support a universal performance ordering between leader-based and leaderless consensus: the measured relationship emerges from the interaction of these factors, and neither observation should be interpreted as a general superiority claim for either protocol family.

The study also illustrates a methodological consideration for empirical consensus research: controlled follow-up experiments, including persistence matching and communication-cost injection, provide useful evidence for determining whether an observed difference is robust to changes in the surrounding implementation environment, and performance is most informative when analysed as a function of controlled system conditions rather than reduced to a single throughput or latency figure.

Several limitations constrain the generalisation of these findings: the experiments were conducted in a single-host containerised environment with operating-system-level network emulation, a limited range of cluster sizes, and a closed-loop workload, and the correctness experiments verified convergence rather than linearizability.

Future work could extend the methodology to multi-host and geographically distributed environments, additional independent implementations, larger replica sets, and workloads with stronger contention and more diverse access patterns. Profiling at the storage, transport, scheduling, and application layers could further separate protocol-level behaviour from implementation overhead.
