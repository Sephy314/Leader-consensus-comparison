\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented an experimental comparison of Raft and EPaxos as representative leader-based and leaderless consensus approaches. Rather than treating a single throughput measurement as representative of a consensus protocol, the evaluation examined throughput, latency, replica scaling, client concurrency, failure recovery, resource utilisation, and communication sensitivity under controlled variations in workload and system conditions.

The workload and scaling experiments show that the measured performance relationship depends on the operating condition. Increasing the replica count reduced throughput for both evaluated implementations, while increasing client concurrency produced a substantially larger throughput increase for EPaxos at high concurrency. The evaluated Raft configuration routes normal requests through an elected leader, whereas EPaxos permits proposal initiation at multiple replicas. This structural difference is consistent with the larger throughput response observed for EPaxos under high concurrency, but the measurements do not establish that distributed proposal processing is the sole cause of the difference. The read/write and conflict experiments further show that workload composition does not necessarily produce a simple or monotonic throughput response for the evaluated HashiCorp Raft and original EPaxos implementations; the nvb EPaxos implementation, by contrast, declined monotonically with the configured conflict fraction over the tested range.

The failure experiments identify a distinct operational consequence of the leader-centred request path. Failure of the Raft leader produced an availability gap of 1.81--4.74\,s, whereas failure of a Raft follower produced gaps below 0.1\,s in the evaluated three-replica configuration. EPaxos replica failure likewise produced short interruptions in the tested scenario. These results show that failure behaviour depends on which replica is removed and how the request-processing path is structured. The election experiment further characterised the Raft recovery process, with the measured number of failed election attempts strongly associated with the observed availability gap. These measurements describe the tested recovery configuration rather than protocol-independent recovery bounds.

The implementation-sensitivity experiments provide the strongest qualification to direct protocol comparisons. The evaluated Raft and EPaxos implementations exhibited substantially different absolute performance and different responses to injected communication cost, including substantial differences between implementations of the same protocol family. Persistence, request processing, transport handling, scheduling, and adapter behaviour can therefore materially affect measured throughput and latency without changing the underlying consensus algorithm. Absolute benchmark results should consequently be interpreted as properties of the evaluated implementation and environment rather than as intrinsic performance constants of Raft or EPaxos.

Taken together, the results do not establish a universal performance ordering between leader-based and leaderless consensus. Instead, they show that the observed relationship depends jointly on consensus structure, implementation, workload, replica count, client concurrency, communication conditions, and failure mode. The evaluated Raft configuration exposes the concentration and failure dependency associated with a leader-centred request path, while the evaluated EPaxos configuration exposes a different trade-off through distributed proposal placement and dependency coordination.

The study is limited by its single-host containerised environment, operating-system-level network emulation rather than a physical network, tested cluster sizes, closed-loop workload model, and the specific implementations evaluated. The correctness experiments additionally establish state convergence under the tested workloads and failures rather than formal consensus correctness or general linearizability. These limitations restrict direct generalisation to production deployments and larger or geographically distributed systems.

Nevertheless, the experimental methodology provides a reproducible basis for studying consensus implementations under controlled changes in workload and system conditions. Future work can extend the comparison to additional independent implementations, larger clusters, multi-host deployments, network-level emulation, detailed profiling, and workloads that exercise stronger ordering and contention conditions. Such extensions would help further distinguish protocol-level effects from implementation-specific behaviour.
