\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study experimentally compared Raft, a leader-based consensus
protocol~\cite{ongaro2014raft}, with EPaxos, a leaderless
protocol~\cite{moraru2013epaxos}. Under the evaluated workloads and
experimental conditions, Raft provided a comparatively simple and stable
coordination structure, while EPaxos achieved higher throughput and lower
latency in several of the evaluated scenarios. The two protocols also
exhibited different failure behaviour under the tested conditions.

The results highlight the costs associated with centralised leadership. In
particular, Raft exhibited a noticeable availability cost when the leader
failed, while increasing the replica count was associated with reduced
throughput for both protocols and a more pronounced increase in latency for
Raft. These observations show that a leader can provide a clear coordination
point for replicated writes, but can also become a concentration point for
processing and a point of disruption when it fails. In contrast, EPaxos
distributes coordination across replicas, reducing its dependence on a single
leader at the cost of a more complex consensus mechanism and higher memory
consumption in the evaluated implementation.

These findings should be interpreted within the scope of the experimental
setup. The compared systems use different implementations, and
implementation-level differences may affect the measured performance: Raft is
represented by the widely deployed HashiCorp Raft
library~\cite{hashicorpraft}, whereas the EPaxos measurements use the research
prototype published with the original protocol~\cite{moraru2013epaxos}.
Furthermore, the experiments were conducted in a controlled local environment
rather than a geographically distributed production deployment. Therefore, the
measured performance and failure behaviour should not be interpreted as
universal characteristics of the protocols in all deployment environments
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

Overall, the results demonstrate a fundamental trade-off between centralised
and distributed coordination in distributed consensus: leader-based
coordination can simplify system behaviour and provide a clear ordering
structure, while leaderless coordination can reduce dependence on a single
coordination point and provide advantages under certain workloads and failure
conditions. The experiments therefore indicate that the practical cost of a
consensus architecture depends not only on its steady-state throughput, but
also on how coordination, resource consumption, scaling, and failure recovery
are distributed across the system.
