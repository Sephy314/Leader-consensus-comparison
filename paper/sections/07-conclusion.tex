\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:conclusion}

This study conducted a controlled experimental comparison of Raft and EPaxos as representative leader-based and leaderless consensus designs. Rather than treating a single benchmark configuration as representative of either protocol family, the study varied replica count, client concurrency, workload composition, conflict ratio, failure mode, communication cost, and persistence configuration. This design was intended to distinguish effects associated with consensus structure from those introduced by implementation and experimental environment.

The results show that the relationship between the evaluated implementations is strongly dependent on operating conditions. Increasing the replica count reduced throughput in both implementations, while the evaluated EPaxos configuration maintained higher throughput under high client concurrency. A Raft-specific scaling experiment further showed that this penalty persisted as concurrency increased, with CPU utilisation increasingly concentrated at the leader. These observations are consistent with the resource concentration introduced by a leader-centred request path, although the experiments do not establish that protocol structure alone accounts for the observed differences. Workload composition also did not produce a uniform response across implementations: only nvb EPaxos exhibited a measurable response to the configured conflict fraction.

The failure experiments reveal another consequence of centralised request processing. In the tested three-replica configuration, leader failure interrupted write processing until a replacement leader was established, whereas follower failure in Raft and replica failure in EPaxos produced substantially shorter interruptions. The duration and number of failed operations varied with the recovery configuration and implementation. These results therefore characterise the observed failure behaviour of the evaluated systems rather than establishing protocol-independent recovery bounds.

The implementation-sensitivity experiments provide an important qualification to direct protocol-level comparisons. Implementations belonging to the same consensus family differed in absolute throughput and latency and did not necessarily respond identically to controlled communication delays. Persistence behaviour, request processing, transport mechanisms, scheduling, and adapter design could materially alter the measured results without changing the underlying consensus algorithm. Consequently, absolute performance measurements should be interpreted as properties of a particular implementation operating in a particular environment, rather than as intrinsic performance constants of Raft or EPaxos.

Taken together, the findings do not support a universal performance ordering between leader-based and leaderless consensus. Instead, the measured relationship emerges from the interaction of these factors. The evaluated Raft implementation demonstrates the operational consequences of concentrating request processing and coordination around a leader, while the evaluated EPaxos implementations demonstrate the potential benefits and costs of distributing proposal processing and dependency coordination across replicas. Neither observation, however, should be interpreted as a general superiority claim for either protocol family.

The study also illustrates a methodological consideration for empirical consensus research. Controlled follow-up experiments, including persistence matching and communication-cost injection, provide useful evidence for determining whether an observed difference is robust to changes in the surrounding implementation environment. This does not eliminate implementation effects, but makes their contribution explicit and limits the risk of attributing implementation behaviour directly to protocol design. Performance is therefore most informative when analysed as a function of controlled system conditions rather than reduced to a single throughput or latency figure, and the combination of protocol-level analysis with implementation-level validation provides a reproducible basis for more careful evaluation of distributed consensus systems.

Several limitations constrain the generalisation of these findings. The experiments were conducted in a single-host containerised environment and used operating-system-level network emulation rather than a physical distributed network. The evaluation covered a limited range of cluster sizes and used a closed-loop workload, while the correctness experiments verified convergence rather than linearizability. In addition, only the specific implementations evaluated in this study can be directly characterised by the reported measurements. These limitations make the results most applicable as controlled comparative evidence rather than as predictions of production or geographically distributed deployments.

Future work could extend the methodology to multi-host and geographically distributed environments, additional independent implementations, larger replica sets, and workloads with stronger contention and more diverse access patterns. Profiling at the storage, transport, scheduling, and application layers could further separate protocol-level behaviour from implementation overhead, testing whether the relationships observed here remain stable as the environment approaches production deployments.
